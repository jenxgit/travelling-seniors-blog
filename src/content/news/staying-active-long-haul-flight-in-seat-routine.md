There is a particular stillness that settles over a cabin somewhere past the second meal service, when the window shades come down and a plane full of strangers agrees, without discussion, to stop moving for a while. It feels like rest. For the body, held in the same slightly reclined position for hour after hour, it is something closer to a held breath. A physiotherapist friend in Singapore once put it to me plainly: the problem with a long-haul flight was never really the flying. It is the stillness.

Australia's geography makes this an unavoidable part of travelling well. Wherever you are departing from — Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Perth — the destinations most of us dream of are rarely a short hop away. A flight to Europe is commonly eighteen to twenty-two hours in the air across two sectors; even the more direct routes to North America or the Middle East run well past twelve. None of what follows is medical advice, and it is not a substitute for a proper conversation with your own GP, particularly if you have a history of blood clots, recent surgery, or a cardiovascular condition — for you, that conversation matters more than anything in this article. But for many of us, a little planned movement is simply good sense, and it is worth understanding why before you understand how.

## Why Movement Matters More Than We Think

Sitting still restricts blood flow through the deep veins of the lower leg, where gravity and a bent hip already have circulation working against them. The calf muscles are sometimes called the body's second heart, because their contraction as we walk helps pump blood back up towards it — a job that a seated, unmoving leg simply cannot do. This is a bigger consideration as we age, when veins lose some of their elasticity and the body's ordinary circulatory adjustments happen a little more slowly than they once did. The good news is that the antidote costs nothing and needs no equipment: it is simply movement, repeated often, in whatever space a coach or economy seat allows.

## A Physiotherapist's Simple In-Seat Routine

The routine I was taught, and have used on every long sector since, takes perhaps three minutes and can be done without disturbing the passenger beside you. Aim to work through it every hour or two you are seated:

- **Ankle circles.** Lift your feet slightly and rotate each ankle slowly, ten times in each direction. This keeps the calf muscles doing their quiet pumping work even while you stay seated.
- **Foot pumps.** With heels on the floor, lift the front of your foot as high as it will go, then reverse — heel up, toes down. Twenty slow repetitions wake the calves properly.
- **Knee lifts.** Seated tall, lift one knee towards your chest as far as is comfortable, hold for a breath, then lower and swap sides. This one also does a small favour for a stiff lower back.
- **Shoulder rolls and neck tilts.** Roll the shoulders back slowly, ten times, then let the ear drift gently towards each shoulder in turn. Hours spent leaning towards a window or an armrest add up in the neck more than most of us expect.
- **Seated marching.** If there is room, lift one foot and then the other in a slow, seated march for thirty seconds. It feels faintly silly and does the job all the same.

None of these movements should cause pain. A physiotherapist's routine is a gentle one by design, and if anything here aggravates a joint or muscle you already manage carefully, it is entirely reasonable to leave it out.

## Getting Up Without Disrupting the Cabin

The in-seat routine matters, but it is not a replacement for standing and walking. Most airlines and health authorities suggest getting up and moving through the cabin roughly once every hour or two on a long sector, and an aisle seat — worth requesting when you book, or asking to swap for once aboard — makes this considerably easier to do without disturbing your row. A short walk to the galley and back, done unhurriedly, is enough; there is no need to make a production of it. I find the quieter stretch of cabin near the rear galleys, once the meal trolleys have cleared, is usually the easiest place to pause and do a few standing calf raises without feeling in anyone's way.

Timing this around the cabin lighting helps too. On an overnight sector, a slow walk during the dim, quiet hours can also serve as a small dose of the movement your body will thank you for at the other end, when the real work of adjusting to a new time zone begins.

## Hydration, Compression, and Listening to Your Body

Cabin air is notably dry, and dehydration makes blood thicker and slower-moving, which rather undoes the good work of your ankle circles. A glass of water every hour you are awake is a reasonable target, poured well before thirst announces itself — thirst is a late signal at the best of times, and cabin air makes it later still. I would gently suggest treating the drinks trolley as a floor, not a ceiling, and asking the crew for water in between passes if you would like it.

Many of my fellow travellers swear by graduated compression socks for long sectors, and the evidence for their role in supporting circulation on long flights is genuinely well regarded — though the correct compression level varies by individual, so it is worth asking your GP or pharmacist which is appropriate for you rather than guessing at the airport newsagent. And through all of this, the same rule applies as it would on any walking trail or wellness routine: ordinary travel fatigue is one thing, but calf pain, swelling, redness, or a leg that feels warm to the touch on arrival is a signal to see a doctor promptly, not to wait it out.

None of this is complicated, and none of it demands much of you beyond a little intention every hour or two. A cabin full of people sitting still is, in its way, an invitation — to be the one person quietly working through ankle circles, glass of water in hand, arriving rather better than most.
