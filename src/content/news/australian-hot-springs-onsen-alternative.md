I have written before about the long and genuinely lovely tradition of *toji* — therapeutic hot spring bathing in Japan — and I stand by every word of it. But a reader wrote to me recently, quite reasonably, to ask whether she really needed twenty-four hours of flying and a jet-lagged first week to get the benefit of sitting in warm, mineral-rich water for an afternoon. The honest answer is no. Australia has its own hot springs, naturally heated and genuinely mineral-rich, and for many of us they are a half-day's drive rather than a half-world away.

None of what follows is medical advice, and it is certainly not a substitute for your own doctor's judgement. Warm water immersion raises the heart rate and causes blood vessels near the skin to widen, which is a pleasant sensation for most of us but a real consideration for anyone with heart disease, uncontrolled blood pressure, or who is pregnant — a conversation with your GP before a hot springs visit is a sensible first stop if any of that applies to you, as is checking with your doctor if you manage a skin condition or reduced sensation in your feet, since some of what I describe below involves water hotter than you might expect.

## Peninsula Hot Springs, Victoria

About ninety minutes south of Melbourne on the Mornington Peninsula, Peninsula Hot Springs draws its water from a bore sunk more than 600 metres into the aquifer below — genuinely geothermal, rather than simply heated on site. Across more than seventy pools spread over 42 acres, the temperatures range from brisk cold plunges through to properly hot soaking pools, with a hilltop pool, a Japanese-inspired cave pool, and a reflexology walk among the more memorable spots. It is, by design, the closest thing Australia has to a proper onsen-style bathing day, cultural borrowings and all.

![An older couple sitting together outdoors, relaxed and unhurried](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1761839257647-df30867afd54?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1600&h=900&q=80 "A hot springs day is as much about the unhurried company as the water itself")

This is also the easiest of the three for anyone with mobility concerns. The main pathways are wheelchair accessible with ramped pool entries, and the team there will talk through specific needs if you call ahead — always worth doing a few days before you visit rather than discovering a limitation on arrival. The adults-only Spa Dreaming Centre is booked and priced separately from general bathing entry, so decide which experience you actually want before you commit to a ticket. As with any public bathing, pace yourself: alternating a warm pool with a cooler one, and taking a proper break with water in hand, keeps the afternoon comfortable rather than overwhelming.

## Yarrangobilly, New South Wales

Deep in the northern reaches of Kosciuszko National Park, the Yarrangobilly thermal pool is a different kind of experience altogether — natural, free to use once you are in the park, and open year-round regardless of the snow on the surrounding peaks. The main pool is a generous 20 metres long and mineral-rich with calcium, sodium and magnesium, fed by a spring that keeps it comfortably warm through every Snowy Mountains winter. A smaller, shallower pool connects to it for anyone who would rather not negotiate the main pool's lack of a shallow end.

The catch, and it is worth knowing before you go rather than discovering it on the day, is the walk: roughly a kilometre each way along the Yarrangobilly River, finishing with a hillier stretch back up to the car park. It is a manageable walk for most of us, but it is not a stroll from the car door, so a decent pair of walking shoes and a sensible pace matter more here than the swim itself. The road in is also a winding mountain drive with patchy phone reception, so check the Kosciuszko National Park vehicle entry requirements and let someone know your plans before you set off, particularly if you are travelling alone.

## Innot Hot Springs, Far North Queensland

On the Atherton Tablelands, roughly between Mount Garnet and Ravenshoe, the springs at Innot are the most unvarnished of the three. The natural spring water bubbling up through Nettle Creek runs between 74 and 85 degrees Celsius at its source — genuinely scalding, and not somewhere to simply wade in. The time-honoured way to enjoy it, as locals have done for generations, is to dig a shallow pool at the creek's edge where the hot spring water mixes with the cooler creek flow, letting you find your own comfortable temperature a hand's width at a time rather than trusting a tap.

This is the spot where I would most urge a little extra caution. Anyone with diabetes-related nerve changes, poor circulation, or reduced sensation in their feet should be especially careful testing the water before settling in, since it is genuinely possible to underestimate how hot a patch of water is until the damage is done — ask your GP about safe water temperatures for your own feet if you have any doubt at all. Going with a companion, moving slowly, and checking current local signage and advice before you dig in are all worth the extra five minutes.

## What the Research Actually Says

I would be doing you a disservice if I oversold the science here. Warm water immersion is well understood to ease muscle tension and, anecdotally, many people with arthritis say a soak leaves their joints feeling looser for a day or two. But the formal research on balneotherapy — bathing in mineral-rich spring water specifically, as distinct from ordinary warm water — remains genuinely mixed; Cochrane's own reviews of hydrotherapy for arthritis describe the existing trials as small and of low certainty, not a verdict either way. Healthdirect's general guidance on managing joint pain lists gentle heat as one reasonable option among several, alongside movement, medication and your GP's own advice, rather than a stand-alone treatment. In other words: a hot springs day is a genuinely pleasant, low-risk way to spend an afternoon for most healthy adults, and it may well leave your joints feeling better for it, but it is a companion to your GP's advice rather than a replacement for it.

## Before You Go

Pack as you would for any day near water: a proper hat and sunscreen even in cooler months, a change of clothes, and plenty of water to drink, since warm bathing is more dehydrating than it feels at the time. Seniors Card holders should always ask about concession pricing at a staffed venue like Peninsula Hot Springs, since the rules vary and it is worth the thirty-second question. And wherever you go, give yourself a quiet afternoon afterwards rather than a long drive home — the same unhurried pace that makes a hot spring worthwhile is worth extending to the rest of the day.

Three different hot springs, three different moods, and not a single long-haul flight among them. Sometimes the thing we travelled the world looking for was closer to home than we thought.
