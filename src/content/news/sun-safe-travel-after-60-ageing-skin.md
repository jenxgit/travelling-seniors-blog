Most of us who grew up in Australia carry a particular kind of sun history on our skin, whether we think about it often or not. Summers at the beach before sunscreen was routine, farm work or outdoor sport in the days before UPF clothing existed, a decade or two when a tan was considered the picture of good health rather than a sign of damage. None of that can be undone, and I don't raise it to cause worry. I raise it because it changes the arithmetic for how we protect our skin now, particularly when a holiday is taking us somewhere the sun is stronger than the one we left at home.

Australia already has among the highest rates of skin cancer in the world, a legacy of that sun exposure layered on our naturally fair population mix. By the time we reach our sixties and seventies, the skin itself has also changed: it is thinner, slower to repair, and less able to mount the kind of immune response that once helped clear away early cellular damage before it became a problem. None of what follows is medical advice, and any mole or skin change that concerns you deserves a proper look from your GP or a dermatologist rather than a guess based on an article. But there is a good deal of sensible planning that can sit alongside that medical care, especially before a trip to somewhere sunnier than home.

## Why the Risk Genuinely Changes With Age

The skin's outer layer thins measurably from around our fifties onward, offering less of a physical barrier between UV radiation and the living cells beneath it. At the same time, years of accumulated sun exposure mean that many of us are carrying pre-cancerous or early cancerous changes that simply haven't become visible yet, a process the Cancer Council and most dermatologists describe as a long latency period between exposure and a diagnosis that can stretch for decades. This is part of why non-melanoma skin cancers, such as basal cell and squamous cell carcinoma, become significantly more common with age, and why melanoma, while it can appear at any age, carries a higher mortality risk when it is diagnosed later in life, partly because the diagnosis itself tends to come later.

A Mediterranean summer or an equatorial Asian destination such as Thailand, Malaysia or Singapore brings genuinely stronger sun than most of an Australian winter, and in the latter case, a UV index that stays high year-round rather than following our familiar seasonal pattern. The extra strength of that sun is not a reason to avoid such a trip. It is a reason to pack and plan a little differently than we might have twenty years ago.

## Before You Go: The Skin Check Worth Booking

A full-body skin check from your GP or a dermatologist, ideally scheduled a few weeks before a big trip rather than the day before you fly, is one of the most useful things an Australian traveller over sixty can do. It gives a professional the chance to examine anything that has changed, and it gives you a clear baseline to compare against when you're home again and checking your own skin after a stretch in strong sun.

Between professional checks, the Cancer Council's "ugly duckling" approach is a reasonable way to look at your own skin at home: rather than trying to memorise detailed criteria, simply notice any spot that looks different from your other moles and freckles, or that has changed in size, shape or colour since you last paid attention. A spot that stands out from the pattern of everything around it is the one worth mentioning to your doctor, not a reason for alarm on its own.

## Packing: Clothing First, Sunscreen Second

Dermatologists increasingly describe sunscreen as the backup plan rather than the main defence, because it needs reapplying every two hours to stay effective and is easy to miss on the back of the neck, the ears, or the part in your hair. A UPF 50+ long-sleeved shirt, a pair of lightweight trousers, and a hat with a genuinely wide brim do their job all day without being reapplied, and they have become far more breathable and travel-friendly than the sun shirts of a decade ago. Look for a hat meeting the Australian/New Zealand standard AS/NZS 4399, with a brim of at least seven and a half centimetres all the way around, which shades the ears and the back of the neck as well as the face.

Where skin is left uncovered, a broad-spectrum sunscreen of SPF 50+ remains essential, reapplied every two hours and immediately after swimming. For a reef or marine destination, a mineral or "reef-safe" formulation protects the coral as well as your skin, and is worth packing from home, since it can be harder to find once you've arrived. A separate SPF lip balm is easy to forget and frequently overlooked, despite the lips being a genuinely common site for sun-related skin cancers in older Australians. UV-protective sunglasses meeting the AS/NZS 1067:2003 standard round out the kit, protecting the thin, sun-sensitive skin around the eyes as well as the eyes themselves.

## Working the Itinerary Around the Sun

In both the Mediterranean in summer and equatorial Asia year-round, the UV index typically peaks between eleven in the morning and three in the afternoon, regardless of how warm or cool the air feels. A simple adjustment that costs nothing is treating that window as indoor time: a gallery, a museum, a long lunch, or a siesta in the shade, with the walking tours, markets and photography saved for the softer light of morning or late afternoon. This does double duty, since it is also when the heat itself is least comfortable for walking any distance.

![A red sunshade on a beach under a bright sky](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1590248452371-3459c0a04286?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1600&h=900&q=80 "Genuine shade, not just a cooling breeze, is what actually reduces UV exposure through the middle of the day")

A patch of genuine shade, under a tree, an awning or a proper beach umbrella, does more to cut UV exposure than most travellers assume, since even a light breeze can make strong sun feel deceptively mild. On cruise itineraries and shore excursions in particular, it's worth asking in advance whether shaded seating is available on tender boats and open-top tour buses, rather than discovering the answer partway through a long day.

## A Word on Melanoma Risk Specifically

Australians travelling to the Mediterranean or to equatorial Asia are sometimes surprised that a destination with less familiar, more intense sun can catch out even careful sun-avoiders, largely because sunburn from a single intense exposure is itself a recognised risk factor for melanoma, distinct from the slower accumulation of damage from years of moderate exposure. A first day or two of acclimatising, building up time outdoors gradually rather than making the most of every daylight hour immediately on arrival, genuinely reduces this risk, and it rarely costs more than a slightly later start to the sightseeing.

If you notice a new mole, a sore that won't heal, or any spot that has changed since you left home, don't wait until you're back in Australia to have it looked at. Most destinations popular with Australian travellers have accessible medical care, and your travel insurer's assistance line can usually point you to an English-speaking doctor or dermatologist quickly if something concerns you while you're away.

None of this is about trading away the joy of a sunny destination for a life spent indoors. It's about packing a little differently, timing the day a little more deliberately, and treating that pre-trip skin check as seriously as you'd treat a dental check before a long flight. The sun that drew you to the Mediterranean or the tropics in the first place is worth enjoying fully, just from a position of having done the groundwork first.
