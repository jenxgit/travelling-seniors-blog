There is a particular satisfaction in watching a continent go by from an armchair, and nowhere is that satisfaction more complete than aboard the Indian Pacific. Over three nights and four days, the train draws a line from Sydney Harbour to the Swan River, and along the way it does something no aircraft ever could: it lets you feel the size of Australia, one slow degree of longitude at a time.

For those considering the journey for the first time, the practical questions tend to matter as much as the romance — which class of service suits you, how far ahead to book, and whether the famous Nullarbor crossing is as featureless as its reputation suggests. Allow me to walk you through it properly.

## The Route and the Rhythm of the Journey

Sydney to Perth runs three nights and four days; the return leg, Perth to Sydney, takes four nights and five days, owing to the timetable rather than the distance. Either way, you are covering some 4,352 kilometres, which places the Indian Pacific among the longest train journeys on earth.

The train departs Sydney and climbs almost immediately into the Blue Mountains before the land opens out toward Broken Hill, where most itineraries include an off-train excursion into the old silver-mining town. From there it is on to Adelaide, then west into South Australia's arid interior and the crossing that gives the journey its name: the Nullarbor Plain, a stretch so flat and treeless that the railway runs dead straight for 478 kilometres between Ooldea and Watson — the longest straight stretch of track in the world. Brief stops at the tiny settlements of Cook and Rawlinna offer a chance to step onto the plain itself, before the line finally curves through Kalgoorlie's goldfields and on to Perth.

What surprised me most on my own first crossing was not the emptiness of the Nullarbor but the company found within the train itself. Long, unhurried afternoons in the lounge car have a way of turning strangers into travelling companions by the second evening.

## Choosing Your Class of Service

The Indian Pacific offers three tiers, and the difference between them is worth understanding before you book, because you will spend a great deal of time in your cabin over four days.

**Gold Service** is the traditional entry point: a comfortable twin or single sleeper cabin that converts from day seating to berths overnight, with shared end-of-carriage bathroom facilities. **Gold Premium Service** adds a private ensuite to a similar cabin footprint. **Platinum Service** is the top tier — larger cabins with a private ensuite, more generous storage, and priority seating at meal times.

One point worth noting: because dining and lounge carriages are allocated by service level, guests travelling in different classes will not be able to move through the train to dine or socialise together. If you are travelling with family or friends in a different tier, plan your onboard time with that in mind.

### Practical Senior Tips:
- **Book cabins in the same carriage, not just the same train**, if you want easy visiting between travelling companions.
- **Luggage allowances differ by class** — Gold Service permits two 30kg checked pieces per person, Gold Premium three at 25kg, and Platinum three at 30kg. Anything beyond that travels at your own arrangement, so pack with the limit in mind.

## Accessibility and Mobility

This is where I am glad to report genuine thoughtfulness in the design of the service. A **Gold Access Cabin** is available for wheelchair users and guests with lower mobility, fitted out accordingly — though it is offered subject to availability and a Fitness to Travel assessment, so it is worth raising your needs with the operator at the time of booking rather than assuming a standard request will cover it. Wheelchairs, walking frames and mobility scooters can be checked in as luggage for the journey.

Do also flag any mobility considerations before you commit to an off-train excursion at Broken Hill, Cook or Rawlinna — the platforms and ground underfoot vary in evenness, and staff can advise which stops suit which level of mobility.

## Booking Windows and What It Costs

Fares move with three seasonal bands: high season covers March and September through November, shoulder season takes in January–February, April–May, August and December, and low season is June and July. As a rule, booking into shoulder or low season and securing your cabin several months out will do more for your fare than any other single decision.

I will be candid about one thing: there is no blanket Seniors Card discount on the Indian Pacific, unlike some regional bus and rail operators. What does turn up periodically are advance-purchase fares aimed specifically at seniors and pensioners, released through travel agents rather than sold as a standing concession — it is worth asking a rail specialist agent to flag these for you rather than checking only the operator's own site.

## Packing for a Continent's Worth of Climate

The Indian Pacific crosses more climate zones than most Australian journeys manage, and it catches first-timers out more than anything else on this list. Adelaide and Perth can sit around 30 degrees in summer, while the Nullarbor, Rawlinna and Cook can drop to around 5 degrees overnight in winter. A layered approach to packing — something warm for the evening lounge car, something light for a daytime stop in Perth — will serve you far better than committing to one season's wardrobe.

### Before You Book:
- Confirm which service level your travelling companions are booking, so you are not separated for meals.
- Ask about the Gold Access Cabin early if mobility is a consideration — it is assessed, not automatic.
- Aim for shoulder or low season if your calendar allows it.
- Pack for a 25-degree swing in temperature, not the one you left home in.
- Ask a rail specialist travel agent about advance-purchase senior fares before assuming none exist.

Few journeys reward patience quite like this one. By the time the Swan River comes into view, you will have crossed a continent slowly enough to remember every part of it.
