A reader wrote to me recently, not for advice before booking, but after. She'd found what looked like a terrific deal on a river cruise through a Facebook ad — a well-known cruise line's name, a photo she recognised from the brochure, a price several hundred dollars under everyone else's. She paid a deposit by bank transfer, because the "agent" on the phone said card payments were "having technical issues that week." Six weeks later the booking didn't exist, the phone number was dead, and the bank transfer, as bank transfers do, had gone somewhere entirely unrecoverable.

Nothing about her story suggested carelessness. The ad looked professional, the price was plausible rather than absurd, and the person on the phone was patient, friendly, and fluent — none of the broken English or garish exclamation marks we were all taught to watch for a decade ago. That's the part worth sitting with: the old advice about spotting a scam by its spelling mistakes is largely obsolete. Scammers now write as well as anyone, sometimes with help from the same generative tools the rest of us use for a cover letter. What hasn't changed is where the actual danger sits — not in the advertisement, but in the moment your money is asked to move somewhere it can't be traced or reversed.

## The One Rule That Catches Almost Everything Else

If I could leave you with a single sentence from this whole article, it's this: **a legitimate Australian travel business will always let you pay by credit card, and will never insist on a bank transfer, cryptocurrency, or a gift card to "lock in" a deal.** Scammers prefer those payment methods for the specific reason that they're difficult or impossible to claw back once sent. A request to pay that way — especially paired with pressure to act "today" or "before the price changes" — is close to a guarantee that something is wrong, regardless of how good everything else about the offer looks.

Everything else in this article is really detail underneath that one rule. But the detail is worth having, because a confident scammer will often let you pay by card too, and simply take the money for a holiday that was never real to begin with.

## Check the Accreditation, Properly

The Australian travel industry runs its own accreditation scheme, ATAS, now administered by the Australian Travel Industry Association (ATIA) — the renamed Australian Federation of Travel Agents, so don't be thrown if you see both names still floating around online. An ATAS-accredited business has met financial and conduct standards set by the industry body and agreed to its code of conduct, which gives you somewhere to lodge a complaint if things go wrong and some assurance the operator is a real, ongoing business rather than a website thrown up last month.

Checking accreditation takes under a minute, and I'd do it before paying any deposit to an operator you haven't dealt with before:

1. Go to **atas.com.au** and search the business name directly, rather than trusting a logo on their own website — logos and badges can simply be copied onto a scam site.
2. If the business claims accreditation but doesn't appear in the search, treat that as a serious red flag rather than an oversight.
3. Note that an old "AFTA" sticker in a shopfront window isn't proof of anything current — the scheme rebranded to ATIA, and accredited businesses were asked to retire the old branding. A current operator should be able to point you to their live ATAS listing without hesitation.

ATAS accreditation isn't a legal requirement to sell travel in Australia, so plenty of perfectly reputable smaller operators won't have it. It's simply one more data point, and an easy one to check, in deciding how much trust an unfamiliar name has earned.

## Reading an Offer Like a Sceptic, Politely

Before you get anywhere near a payment page, run the offer itself through a few quick checks:

- **Compare the price against at least two other sources** for the same cruise, tour, or hotel package — the cruise line's own site, a second travel agent, a comparison site. A price that's a little cheaper is normal competition. A price that's dramatically cheaper than everyone else for an identical itinerary is the detail scammers rely on you not checking.
- **Search the business name plus the word "scam" or "complaint"** before you book. It sounds blunt, but five minutes of searching surfaces almost every operator with a real pattern of problems.
- **Look up the ABN** on the Australian Business Register (abr.business.gov.au) and confirm it belongs to the business named on the site, not to an unrelated company in a different industry entirely.
- **Be wary of an offer that found you**, rather than one you went looking for — an unsolicited email, text, or social media ad for a deal that seems to know rather a lot about your travel habits. Genuine last-minute cruise and tour discounts exist, but they're far more often found by searching than by being targeted.
- **Notice the urgency.** "This price expires at midnight," "only two cabins left," and "you must transfer the deposit today to hold this fare" are pressure tactics as much as they're sales tactics, and real operators generally survive you taking a day to think it over.

## If You've Already Paid: What a Chargeback Can and Can't Do

This is where paying by credit card earns its keep. If you paid by credit card and the operator turns out to be fraudulent, or simply never delivers the booking, you can ask your card issuer for a **chargeback** — a reversal of the transaction, handled through the bank rather than the merchant. It isn't automatic and it isn't guaranteed, but it's a real avenue, and banks are generally used to handling exactly this kind of travel dispute.

A few things worth knowing before you need this:

1. **Act quickly.** Contact your bank as soon as you suspect something is wrong, rather than waiting to see if the problem resolves itself. Most card schemes have a window for raising a dispute, and it isn't indefinite.
2. **Keep everything** — the booking confirmation, every email, screenshots of the website and the advertisement, and a note of any phone conversations with dates. A chargeback claim is won on paperwork.
3. **A bank transfer offers essentially none of this protection.** Once funds leave your account by direct transfer, the bank can attempt to recall them, but success depends on the money still sitting in the receiving account, which, in a scam, it very often isn't by the time anyone notices.
4. **Cryptocurrency and gift cards offer none at all.** There's no issuer to appeal to and no dispute process — which is precisely why scammers ask for them.

If a chargeback isn't available or doesn't succeed, report the matter to Scamwatch (scamwatch.gov.au) and, if money has already moved, to your bank's fraud team and to ReportCyber. It won't always recover the money, but it feeds the pattern-matching that eventually gets fraudulent operators and fake sites taken down, and it may help someone else avoid the same letter I opened this article with.

## A Practical Pre-Deposit Checklist

Before any deposit leaves your account for an operator you haven't used before, work through this list:

| Check | What you're looking for |
|---|---|
| Payment method offered | Credit card available, no insistence on bank transfer, crypto or gift cards |
| ATAS accreditation | Search the business name directly at atas.com.au |
| Price comparison | Checked against at least one other agent or the supplier's own site |
| Business search | Name searched alongside "scam" or "complaint" |
| ABN check | Matches the business named, via abr.business.gov.au |
| Contact details | A real Australian phone number and street address, not a contact form alone |
| Urgency pressure | None that can't survive you sleeping on it overnight |

None of these checks take more than a few minutes individually, and together they take less time than most of us spend deciding which cabin category to book. That's really the whole point: the effort required to avoid this kind of loss is tiny compared with the effort required to recover from one.

## The Bottom Line

A good travel deal and a fake one can look identical right up until the moment you're asked to pay in a way you can't undo. Keep the payment on a credit card, check the accreditation before you trust the badge, and treat any pressure to act immediately as information in itself. Do that, and the "deal" that doesn't survive those few minutes of scrutiny was never really a deal — it was a withdrawal, dressed up as a holiday.
