There are journeys that belong to literature before they belong to the traveller, and a Nile cruise is chief among them. Agatha Christie wrote her passengers into a paddle steamer moored below Aswan; a great many of us have wanted to sit on that same deck ever since, glass in hand, watching temples slide past in the late afternoon light. What has changed is not the river, which does exactly as it has always done, but what now waits at the other end of the itinerary. The Grand Egyptian Museum, twenty years and reportedly over a billion dollars in the making, had its full public opening on 1 November 2025, and 2026 is the first complete year in which a Nile cruise and a proper visit to the museum can be planned as one considered holiday rather than two separate trips.

## Twenty Years in the Making, and Finally Finished

The Grand Egyptian Museum sits at Giza, close enough to the pyramids that both can reasonably occupy the same day, and it is now the largest museum in the world devoted to a single civilisation. Its great achievement, and the reason it is worth building an itinerary around, is that Tutankhamun's full tomb collection — more than five thousand objects — is displayed together for the first time since Howard Carter opened the burial chamber in 1922. Earlier visitors to Cairo saw a fraction of this in the older, considerably more cramped Egyptian Museum in Tahrir Square. This is a different order of experience entirely.

Tickets are timed-entry and sold online only, through the museum's own booking platform rather than at the gate, and I would treat this as a firm instruction rather than a suggestion — a wasted morning in Cairo is not easily recovered from a cruise schedule that moves on without you. The complex opens at 8:30am and the galleries run to 6pm most days, later on Wednesdays and Saturdays, and allowing three to four unhurried hours for the main halls is realistic rather than generous. Prices and exact hours are worth confirming close to your departure date, as both have shifted more than once since the trial opening in 2024.

## Where the Museum Fits Into a Nile Itinerary

Almost every Nile cruise begins or ends in Cairo, which makes the museum a natural bookend rather than a detour. I would allow two to three nights in Cairo before or after the river portion of the trip — one day for the Grand Egyptian Museum and the pyramids of Giza together, since they sit within sight of one another, and at least half a day more for the older treasures still held in the Tahrir Square museum and for Old Cairo's markets and churches, which reward a slower pace than most coach tours allow them.

The river cruise itself typically runs between Luxor and Aswan, a stretch of some 220 kilometres lined with the temples that made this journey famous long before Christie ever set pen to paper: Karnak and Luxor Temple at one end, Philae and the High Dam at the other, with Edfu and Kom Ombo generally called at along the way. A standard sailing runs three to four nights one-way, or about a week if your ship returns the way it came; a great many Australian operators now bundle this into a longer ten-to-fourteen-night package that adds the Cairo stay, internal flights between Cairo and Luxor or Aswan, and sometimes a few days in Jordan besides.

## Choosing Your Ship

The mainstream Nile fleet consists of what the industry rather plainly calls "floating hotels" — five-star vessels carrying anywhere from 60 to over 100 cabins, with a pool deck, a single main dining room, nightly entertainment and, on the better ships, a lift between decks. These are comfortable, well-run and the sensible default for most travellers, and cabin standards on the newer vessels are genuinely high: private balconies, good air-conditioning against the desert heat, and a standard of dining well above what the word "cruise" might lead you to expect.

For a smaller and more romantic alternative, a **dahabiya** is worth knowing about: a traditional twin-masted sailing boat, carrying as few as eight to twelve guests, without a fixed engine schedule to keep and with a character closer to a private yacht than a resort. They cost more per night, move at the whim of the wind rather than a timetable, and are not the right choice if a lift or a full medical bay matters to your travelling party — but for those who find the larger ships a little impersonal, it is the closest thing left to the Nile that Christie's characters actually sailed.

### Practical Senior Tips:
- **Ask specifically whether your ship has a lift between decks** — a number of otherwise excellent mid-range vessels do not, and cabin allocation on the lower deck may be worth requesting if stairs are a concern.
- **A dahabiya suits an able, unhurried traveller better than one who needs onboard medical support** — the larger five-star ships carry more infrastructure for exactly that reason.
- **Request a cabin away from the engine and anchor points** on any vessel, as on any ship, if a quiet night's sleep matters to you.

## Accessibility, Honestly Considered

I would rather tell you plainly than let a glossy brochure do it for me: accessibility in Egypt is not what it is on a modern European river ship, and any operator worth booking with will say the same. Embarkation and disembarkation at some ports involves steps, and on busy days a ship may be moored two or three vessels out from the quay, meaning a walk across a neighbouring boat's deck to reach your own gangway. Adapted, air-conditioned transfer vehicles are now standard with reputable operators, which helps considerably on land.

The temples themselves are the greater challenge. Karnak, Luxor Temple and the others were built for a great many purposes, and universal accessibility was not among them; the more established tour operators now choose routes through the most accessible sections and provide ramps and porterage where the ground itself will not allow a wheelchair through unassisted, but a number of areas remain out of reach regardless of assistance offered. If mobility is a genuine consideration for you or a travelling companion, raise it directly and specifically with the cruise operator before booking, ask what level of physical activity each shore excursion actually requires, and do not accept "easy" as an answer without asking what it means in practice at each individual site.

## Booking Windows and the Australian Season Advantage

The Nile cruising season runs October through April, and this is not a minor preference but close to a necessity: summer temperatures along the river regularly exceed 40 degrees, and touring a sun-exposed temple complex at that heat is unwise at any age. Conveniently for Australians, this window sits across our autumn, winter and spring, making an Egypt itinerary a genuine escape from the cold rather than a trade of one set of seasons for another.

Within that window, December through February is the most heavily booked, partly on temperature and partly on the Australian and European school holiday calendars, so cabins and the better dahabiyas go early and at a premium. October–November and March–April offer very similar weather with noticeably thinner crowds at both the temples and the new museum, and I would steer most readers toward those shoulder months if your own calendar allows the flexibility. As with any high-demand itinerary, booking six to nine months ahead secures both the cabin category and the fare you actually want, rather than whatever remains close to departure.

## Getting There and What to Budget

There is no direct flight from Australia to Cairo, and there will not be one for the foreseeable future; expect one stop, most commonly through Doha with Qatar Airways, Dubai with Emirates, Abu Dhabi with Etihad, or Singapore, with total travel time from the eastern capitals running from roughly 21 to 24 hours depending on the connection and layover. Book this leg with as much care as the cruise itself — a long layover with a bed and a shower on offer will serve you far better after an overnight flight than a fast connection that leaves you touring the pyramids on no sleep at all.

Australian passport holders currently have the choice of a visa on arrival or an e-visa applied for online in advance; either way, a passport valid for at least six months beyond your travel dates and evidence of onward travel are standard requirements, and visa fees and conditions are worth reconfirming close to departure, as they have moved before. Comprehensive travel insurance that explicitly covers medical evacuation is not optional in my view, given the distances involved should anything go wrong.

As for cost, a full Egypt package of nine to thirteen nights — Cairo, the Grand Egyptian Museum, and a Nile cruise, with internal flights included — runs broadly from around $3,700 to $5,800 per person at the more economical end, $7,500 to $12,500 for a well-appointed mid-range sailing, and upward of $17,000 for the small luxury ships and dahabiyas, all in Australian dollars and all before your international airfare. These are indicative bands rather than quotes; ask any operator to itemise exactly what is included, since meals, all shore excursions, visa costs and international flights are handled very differently from one package to the next.

### Before You Book:
- Book Grand Egyptian Museum tickets online, timed-entry, well ahead — there is no walk-up option.
- Allow two to three nights in Cairo either side of the cruise for the museum, the pyramids and Old Cairo.
- Choose October–November or March–April over the peak December–February window for lighter crowds at similar temperatures.
- Ask specifically about lifts, gangway arrangements and shore-excursion terrain if mobility is any consideration at all.
- Confirm visa requirements and take out travel insurance with medical evacuation cover before you fly.

I have wanted to see Tutankhamun's collection whole, in one hall, since I first read about the discovery of his tomb as a girl. That it can now be done in the same fortnight as a sail down the Nile itself feels, frankly, like rather a marvellous coincidence of timing — and one well worth building a 2026 itinerary around.
