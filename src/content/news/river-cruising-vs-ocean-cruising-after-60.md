Readers write to me more often about this question than almost any other: should the next cruise be along a river or across an ocean? Both promise unpacking once and waking somewhere new, but the resemblance rather ends there. The two are different holidays wearing the same word, and the difference matters more after 60 than it did at 30, when a tender boat or a long corridor was simply an inconvenience rather than a genuine consideration.

## The Shape of the Ship, the Shape of the Day

An ocean ship is a small floating city. The larger lines now carry anywhere from 2,000 to well over 5,000 passengers, with theatres, multiple dining rooms, pools and a day's worth of activity that never touches land at all. A river ship, by contrast, typically carries somewhere between 100 and 190 passengers, is long and low to fit under the bridges of the Rhine, Danube or Douro, and has no pool deck to speak of because the ship itself is barely wider than a suburban street.

That difference in scale sets the rhythm of the whole trip. An ocean itinerary tends to mix full days at sea with a handful of port calls, giving you real choice each morning about whether to go ashore at all. A river itinerary moves every night and moors in a new town most mornings, often right in the centre of it — you can typically walk off the gangway and be standing in a market square within five minutes. If you prefer a day of genuine rest between excursions, ocean cruising gives you that more naturally. If you would rather see something different every single day and don't mind the packing being effectively continuous, a river cruise delivers more destinations per week than almost any other form of travel.

## Motion, Mobility and Getting Ashore

This is the consideration I raise first with anyone over 60, because it is the one most easily overlooked when a brochure is doing the talking. Ocean ships are fitted with stabilisers and handle open water well, but rough seas do happen, and a ship that size still moves. River ships, sailing on inland waterways, experience essentially no swell at all — a genuine point in their favour for anyone prone to seasickness or unsteady on their feet.

The more important difference, though, is how you actually get ashore. River ships dock directly against the quay in almost every port, so disembarkation is a level gangway and nothing more. Ocean ships, particularly at smaller or shallower ports, frequently anchor offshore and ferry passengers in by tender boat — a genuine step down into a moving vessel that can be difficult, and is sometimes suspended altogether in choppy conditions, leaving a port missed entirely. If mobility is any consideration at all, ask your cruise line directly, port by port, which calls are tender ports on your specific itinerary before you book.

### Practical Senior Tips:
- **Ask for the tender-port list by name**, not just "how many tender ports," since some itineraries have two and others have eight.
- **River ships have no lifts to the top deck on some older vessels** — if a cabin near the lift matters to you, confirm which deck the lift actually reaches.
- **Request a cabin away from the engine room and anchor** on a river ship, where noise carries more than the brochure photos suggest.

## Cabins, Balconies and Where You'll Actually Spend Your Time

Ocean cabins range enormously, from an interior room with no window at all to a suite with a wraparound veranda, and the sheer number of categories can be its own kind of overwhelming. River cabins are more uniform — most modern vessels offer a French balcony (a floor-to-ceiling glass door that opens, without a step-out platform) as standard, with a smaller number of true walk-out balcony suites toward the upper deck. Given how much of a river day is spent watching the bank slide past from your own room, I would not skimp on this: a French balcony over an ordinary window is, to my mind, the single best-value upgrade in river cruising.

## Dining, Company and the Size of Your World

Ocean ships offer choice: multiple restaurants, specialty dining, room service at any hour, and the option to disappear into a crowd of thousands if that suits your mood. River ships offer familiarity instead — one main dining room, open seating, and a passenger list small enough that you will likely know most fellow travellers by the third evening. Neither is better; it depends whether you find comfort in anonymity or in company. I will say that solo travellers often prefer the river option for exactly this reason — it is harder to feel lost in a room of 120 than a room of 1,200.

## What It Costs in Australian Dollars

River cruising is often assumed to be the pricier of the two, and per night, it usually is — a European river cruise commonly runs from roughly $450 to $700 per person per night, against an ocean cruise that can start well under $200 per night in an interior cabin on a mainstream line. But the comparison is not quite fair on its face: river fares typically include most or all shore excursions, all beverages, and Wi-Fi, where ocean fares on mainstream lines usually charge separately for all three. Once you add those back in, the gap narrows considerably, and for a traveller who intends to go ashore at every port and have a glass of wine with dinner, a river cruise can end up costing little more than an equivalent ocean holiday once the extras are tallied.

Single supplements are worth flagging on both: they typically run from 50 to 100 percent of the twin fare, though a genuine single cabin, without a supplement at all, is becoming more common on newer river vessels — worth asking about by name rather than assuming one doesn't exist.

## Booking Windows and the Australian Season Advantage

For river cruising in Europe, the high season runs June through August — which happens to be our winter, so escaping the cold is very much part of the appeal, alongside long daylight hours on deck. Booking 9 to 12 months ahead generally secures both the better cabin categories and the early-booking fares that most river lines release well before other discounts appear. Ocean cruising has a wider set of seasons depending on the region — Mediterranean summer, Caribbean winter, or closer to home, Australian and New Zealand coastal sailings that run through our own summer — so the booking calendar depends rather more on where you are headed.

### Before You Book:
- Ask for the tender-port count on any ocean itinerary you're considering, not just the port list.
- Choose a French balcony or better on a river ship if watching the passing bank matters to you.
- Add beverages, Wi-Fi and excursions back into an ocean quote before comparing it to a river fare.
- Book river cruising 9 to 12 months ahead for the best cabin and fare combination.
- Ask about a genuine single cabin before accepting a single supplement as inevitable.

Neither is the more sophisticated choice — only the better fit for a particular traveller, at a particular stage. I have loved both, for entirely different reasons, and I suspect you will too, once you know which questions to ask before you sign the booking form.
