I have crossed Bass Strait more times than I can comfortably count, and I will confess a certain fondness for the old Spirit of Tasmania I and II — scuffed carpets and all. So it is with some relief, after a saga that has run since 2018, that I can finally report the replacements are not merely promised but actually here. Spirit of Tasmania IV reached her home port of Devonport for the first time in August, and her sister ship, Spirit of Tasmania V, has been waiting in Australian waters since April. Both are scheduled to begin carrying passengers on the Devonport–Geelong run by the end of this month.

For readers who haven't followed the long and occasionally farcical story: the new ships were ordered from a Finnish shipyard, delivered years ago, and then left idling — one of them famously mothballed at a Scottish berth for the best part of a year while the dedicated terminal back home was built. That terminal, originally budgeted at $90 million, ultimately cost in the order of $493 million. I mention this not to dwell on it, but because it explains why so many of you have written to me asking whether the "new ships" are actually, finally, real. They are. The practical question now is what the upgrade means for you, your car, and — for a good many of my readers — your caravan.

## What's Actually Bigger

The new vessels are not a cosmetic refresh of the old ones; they are substantially larger ships. Spirit of Tasmania IV and V run 212 metres long against the previous 195, are six metres wider in the beam, and stand four metres taller. The figure that will matter most to grey nomads is vehicle space: lane metres for cars and freight rise from 2,565 to 4,098, an increase of around 60 per cent. Anyone who has sat on TT-Line's caravan waitlist in recent years — and I know a great many of you have — will recognise immediately why that matters. It will not eliminate the waitlist overnight, particularly while sailings are being released in limited early stages, but it is the first structural fix this route has had in years rather than another deck rearrangement squeezing a few hundred extra vehicles in at the margins.

Passenger accommodation has grown by a similar margin: 1,138 berths across 301 cabins, against 750 berths in 222 cabins on the current ships. For the first time, TT-Line is also introducing pet cabins, so travellers who have previously had to choose between the dog and the ferry will have a proper alternative. Family cabins and business recliners round out the new categories, though not all of these were available in the first round of ticket releases — more on that below.

### What I'd Flag Before You Book:
- **The vehicle deck increase is real, but gradual.** Early sailings are released in limited numbers, so book your vehicle space as soon as a date opens rather than waiting to see what else comes available.
- **Pet cabins are new territory for this route.** Confirm directly with TT-Line what's required for your animal — crate, vaccination paperwork, access to the cabin during the 9 to 11 hour crossing — before you assume it works the same as a pet-friendly hotel.
- **Wheelchair-accessible seating is built in, not retrofitted.** The new recliner lounges include dedicated wheelchair spaces among both the executive and standard categories, which is worth knowing if accessible seating has been a frustration on the older ships.

## Classes of Service, and Which One Suits an Older Traveller

The recliner lounges remain the budget option, now split between 47 executive recliners and a considerably larger standard section. They suit a shorter daylight crossing well enough, but I would gently steer anyone over 60 towards booking a proper cabin for an overnight sailing. The crossing itself hasn't shortened — it is still a 242-kilometre, nine-to-eleven-hour passage — and a cabin with a door that closes and a bed that stays still is, to my mind, worth the modest difference in fare on a route that, unusually for Australian domestic travel, still operates as a genuine overnight sea voyage rather than a day excursion. With more than half again as many cabins now available as on the old ships, your chances of securing one even in these early, limited releases are considerably better than they would once have been.

The vessels have also been given an enhanced hull and larger stabiliser fins specifically for Bass Strait's reputation for choppier crossings, which should mean a steadier night for anyone who has previously found the strait a touch unkind to an uneasy stomach.

## Booking in Stages

Tickets for the new ships are being released in stages rather than all at once. The first stage covers sailings from 31 October through to 12 December 2026, with cabins and recliners broadly similar to the current ships, plus the benefit of the extra vehicle space. Pet cabins, family cabins and business recliners are following in a later stage, with TT-Line indicating these will progressively come on sale. From January 2027, the schedule is expected to return to the line's usual rolling eleven-month booking window, which is the point at which I'd suggest locking in a cabin for any sailing timed around school holidays or peak caravanning season.

If you already hold a booking on one of the current ships during the transition period, there is no need to panic — both existing vessels continue sailing as normal, and TT-Line has said it will contact anyone whose booking is affected by the changeover with options to amend or cancel.

### Before You Book:
- Check whether your preferred sailing date falls within the current release stage before you get your heart set on a particular cabin category.
- Book vehicle space — car or caravan — as early as the booking window allows; this remains the tightest resource on the route even with the extra lane metres.
- If travelling with a pet for the first time on this route, confirm the pet cabin's practical arrangements directly with TT-Line rather than assuming.
- Ask specifically about wheelchair-accessible recliner or cabin availability if this applies to you — don't assume it's the same limited allocation as on the older ships.

## Does the Ferry Still Beat Flying?

This is the question I'm asked almost as often as the cabin question, and the honest answer continues to depend on who's travelling. For a couple heading over for a week without a vehicle to speak of, flying to Hobart or Launceston and hiring a small car at the other end is typically the cheaper route by some margin — indicative figures put flying-and-hiring anywhere from roughly $150 to $650 ahead of ferry-and-own-car for a week's crossing, once fuel and onboard meals are counted. The calculation flips, though, once you're a family of four, travelling with a caravan, or simply unwilling to leave the dog behind: one vehicle fare on the ferry replaces four separate airfares and their baggage charges, and a worked mid-season example for a family of four has the ferry coming out roughly $270 ahead of the flying-and-hiring alternative across a full week away, accommodation included. Fares on both sides move with the season and the fuel price, so treat these as a guide for the sums you should do yourself rather than a fixed rule, and always check current quotes before you commit either way.

For my own part, I confess the calculation was never purely financial. There is something the budget airlines cannot replicate in watching the Tasmanian coast appear from a cabin window at first light, having slept the crossing rather than endured it. The old ships gave us that experience for a long time, imperfectly. I am rather looking forward to seeing whether the new ones can do it better.
