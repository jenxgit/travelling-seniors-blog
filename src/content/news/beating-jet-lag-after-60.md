There is a particular disorientation that arrives not on the plane but two or three days after it, when the body insists on being wide awake at three in the morning and drowsy through the middle of a museum visit. In my years writing about wellness and travel, I have come to think of jet lag less as tiredness and more as a kind of quiet argument between the body's internal clock and the place it has just landed. The clock is not wrong, exactly — it is simply still running on Melbourne time while the light outside says Lisbon.

Australia's distance from almost everywhere it wants to go makes this an unusually large argument. A flight to Europe or North America typically crosses eight to eleven time zones, more than enough to leave the body's circadian rhythm — the roughly 24-hour internal cycle that governs sleep, appetite and alertness — genuinely confused for several days. None of what follows is medical advice, and it is not a substitute for a conversation with your own doctor, particularly if you manage a health condition on a fixed medication schedule, as several diabetes and heart medications need their timing adjusted carefully around time zone changes, a conversation worth having with your GP well before you fly. But for the ordinary disorientation most of us feel, there is a good deal we can do for ourselves, and it starts with understanding what the body is actually reacting to.

## Why the Body Struggles to Keep Up

The circadian rhythm is set largely by light. A cluster of cells behind the eyes reads the brightness and colour of the light around us and uses it to keep the body's master clock roughly aligned with the day outside — releasing the hormone melatonin as darkness falls to encourage sleep, and suppressing it again as morning light appears. Flying across many time zones in a matter of hours outpaces what that system was built to handle; the body simply cannot reset itself in a single sitting the way an aircraft clock can. As we age, sleep tends to become lighter and more easily disrupted in general, which is one reason jet lag can feel more pronounced at seventy than it did at forty — not a flaw in the traveller, just a body with less slack to absorb a sudden six-hour shift.

## Adjusting Before You Fly

The most effective jet lag strategy begins several days before departure, quietly and without drama. Shifting your bedtime by twenty or thirty minutes a night in the direction of your destination — earlier for an eastbound flight to, say, the United States, later for a westbound one to Europe — gives the body a head start rather than asking it to make the whole adjustment on arrival. Getting outside in the morning light during these lead-up days reinforces the shift; a short walk with a cup of tea in hand does more for your internal clock than it might seem. If your travel involves early-morning departures from Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane or Perth, it is worth building a night of genuinely good sleep into the two days before you fly rather than relying on the flight itself to provide it — a poor night before departure tends to compound rather than cancel out.

## Managing the Flight Itself

Once aboard, the most useful thing you can do is decide, early, what time zone you are now living in — and commit to it. If it is night-time at your destination, even mid-afternoon by the cabin clock, that is a reasonable cue to dim your screen, decline the second coffee, and try to rest. Melatonin supplements are popular among long-haul travellers for exactly this purpose, and while the evidence for modest, well-timed doses is reasonably encouraging, dosing and timing genuinely matter and interact with some medications, so this is worth discussing with your GP or pharmacist before you go rather than experimenting at thirty thousand feet. Hydration deserves its usual mention: cabin air is dry, and dehydration tends to make every symptom of jet lag feel a little worse, so a steady glass of water each hour you are awake is a reasonable target, alcohol and excess caffeine kept to a minimum given how much they can unsettle sleep on a schedule that is already under strain.

## The First Few Days on the Ground

Resist, if you can, the instinct to retreat to a dark hotel room the moment you land. Daylight is the fastest way to tell your body it has genuinely arrived, and a gentle walk in the afternoon sun on your first day does more to reset the internal clock than an early nap, however tempting the nap may be. Where a short nap is unavoidable, keeping it under thirty minutes and before mid-afternoon local time tends to prevent it from working against that night's sleep. Most travellers find their circadian rhythm settles at a rate of roughly one day per time zone crossed, which is worth knowing simply so you can plan a gentler first two or three days on the itinerary — the museum, the long lunch and the layered conversation with old friends tend to be better enjoyed once the fog has genuinely lifted.

## A Few Practical Notes for the Trip Home

The return journey deserves the same planning as the outbound one, and Australians travelling home from Europe or North America often find the eastbound leg harder on the body's clock than the trip over, since it generally asks the body to adjust in the direction it finds least natural. Building a full rest day into your itinerary immediately after you land at home — before returning to ordinary commitments — is a small kindness that pays off. And as with any travel wellness routine, ordinary fatigue is one thing, but persistent insomnia, unusual heart rhythm changes, or any symptom that concerns you after a long trip is a good reason to check in with your GP rather than simply waiting it out.

Jet lag is not something to be defeated so much as managed with a little foresight — a nudge to the bedtime clock before you leave, a walk in the daylight when you arrive, and patience with a body that is, in its own unhurried way, catching up to where you already are.
