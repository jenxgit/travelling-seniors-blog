A reader wrote to me last year about a connecting flight through a large European hub, booked with "assistance requested" ticked in an online form and nothing more. She is blind, travels confidently, and had every reason to expect that ticking a box meant something had actually been arranged. It hadn't. No one met her at the gate, the connection was tight, and she made it only because a fellow passenger walked her across the terminal. The airline wasn't being deliberately unhelpful — the request had simply vanished into a system that, as far as I can tell, nobody on the ground that day was reading. The lesson she took from it, and the one I'd like to save you the trouble of learning the hard way, is that "requesting assistance" and "assistance being arranged" are two different things, and the gap between them is where a low-vision traveller's day goes wrong.

None of what follows requires apologising for needing help, or over-explaining yourself to anyone. It requires asking a short list of specific questions, in good time, of the right person — and getting an answer you can actually point to later if the day doesn't go to plan.

## Telling the Airline What You Actually Need

Most airlines' websites let you tick "special assistance" at booking, but a tick box is not the same as a confirmed arrangement. Qantas, for instance, asks that you advise them at the time of booking if you want Meet and Assist guidance to and from the gate, and that pre-ordered special meals go in no later than 24 hours before departure. A Service Dog traveller is asked to contact the airline directly "for assistance with your specific needs" rather than relying on the booking form alone. Other airlines run broadly similar systems under different names, and the pattern holds everywhere: the booking form starts the request, a phone call to the airline's special assistance line confirms it.

| What you need | What to ask for specifically | When to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Guidance through the terminal and to your seat | "Meet and Assist" (or that airline's equivalent) | At booking, then re-confirm 48–72 hours before departure |
| A braille or large-print safety card | Confirmation one is carried on your specific aircraft | At check-in or boarding, since availability is "on request" rather than universal |
| Priority boarding and an individual safety briefing | Pre-boarding and a verbal briefing from crew | At booking; most carriers do this as standard once you're flagged in the system |
| Travelling with a guide or assistance dog | Cabin travel approval and any paperwork the carrier requires | As early as possible — ideally the moment you know your dates |
| A seating request | Avoid the exit row (you won't be offered one on safety grounds anyway) and ask about proximity to the lavatory | At booking |

The habit worth building is simple: after you've made the request online, ring the airline's special assistance line, ask them to read back what's on file for your booking, and note the day, time and the name of whoever you spoke to. If the day goes wrong despite that, you have something concrete to point to — which, in my experience, gets problems fixed faster than a frustrated conversation at the gate ever does.

Your cane or walking frame can usually travel in the cabin if it folds to fit; if it can't, it goes in the hold and you're offered wheelchair assistance to and from the gate instead, so ask which applies to your specific equipment before you pack it.

## Audio-Described Tours: Ask Before You Land, Not After

Here is the detail that catches out even well-organised travellers: audio-described tours at major museums and galleries are very often scheduled sessions on specific dates, not an on-demand service you can request on the morning you turn up. Queensland's QAGOMA, the Art Gallery of South Australia and Auckland Art Gallery all run dedicated tours for visitors with low or no vision tied to particular exhibitions and calendar dates, and the pattern is the same at major international institutions — a touch tour or audio-described session is typically booked days or weeks ahead, not walked into.

The practical consequence is that the audio-described tour schedule should shape when you visit a gallery, rather than being squeezed in around an itinerary already fixed for other reasons. Before you finalise dates for a destination with a "must-see" museum or gallery, check that institution's access or accessibility page specifically (not just its general "plan your visit" page, which rarely mentions audio description at all), and book the session as firmly as you'd book an opera ticket. Many galleries also offer a touch tour alongside the audio description, where supervised handling of selected objects or replicas is part of the experience — worth asking about even if it isn't advertised prominently.

![Interior of a museum gallery with classical statues and reliefs](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1768288915471-d732a48b5f73?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1600&h=900&q=80 "A gallery that looks like this to a sighted visitor can still be a rich, detailed experience with the right audio description booked ahead")

A smaller, practical point: do a dry run at a gallery back home before you go. The Art Gallery of South Australia, QAGOMA and most state galleries run access programs regularly, and testing out an audio-described tour locally — in familiar surroundings, in English, with no jet lag — tells you a great deal about what works for you before you rely on an equivalent overseas.

## Guide Dogs Abroad: The Paperwork Nobody Mentions

If you're travelling with a trained guide or assistance dog, there are two separate sets of rules to think about, and conflating them is where people come unstuck: the entry rules of the country you're visiting, and the rules for bringing your own dog back home to Australia afterwards. Most advice online focuses on the first and glosses over the second, which is the one with the longer lead time.

The Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry treats a returning Australian assistance dog as an import, the same as any other dog re-entering the country, with a few adjustments for working animals. The essentials, as the department currently sets them out:

1. **An import permit, applied for through BICON, is required for dogs returning from most countries.** The application needs medical evidence establishing why you need the dog and evidence of its training — gather both well before you apply, not the week you're meant to travel.
2. **The whole process can take around six months or more**, involving multiple veterinary visits, tests and treatments, with the health certificate completed by an official government vet in the country you're departing from — not just any local vet.
3. **Trips under 45 days may qualify for reduced testing and treatment requirements** — but you need to say so explicitly on the permit application, it isn't applied automatically.
4. **New Zealand and Norfolk Island are the exceptions**: dogs returning from either need no import permit and no post-arrival quarantine.
5. **Every other country is sorted into a risk group**, and which group your departure country falls into determines what happens on arrival — a compliant dog from a "Group 1" country can generally leave the port of arrival straight away, while dogs from higher-risk Group 2 or 3 countries face quarantine conditions set out in the permit and aren't released without the department's sign-off.
6. **Assistance dogs must fly, not cruise.** International cruise travel with a guide dog is not permitted, which matters if a cruise was ever part of your plan.
7. **Notify the department's regional office at least three working days before you arrive back in Australia**, even once the permit itself is sorted.

None of this is a reason not to travel with your dog — thousands of Australians do it successfully every year — but it is a reason to start the process the day you commit to international dates, not the month before. If any of the detail above looks like it might apply differently to your situation, the department's own advice is to contact them directly before you assume anything, on 1800 900 090 within Australia.

Separately, and well before any of this, check your destination country's own entry requirements for assistance animals through that country's embassy or consulate — the paperwork to get the dog in is entirely distinct from the paperwork to get it home again, and both need sorting on their own timeline.

## A Practical Pre-Booking Checklist

Before you confirm flights, dates or a gallery-heavy itinerary, get clear answers to each of these:

1. Has your airline confirmed — by phone, with a name and reference attached, not just an online tick box — Meet and Assist, your seating preference, and braille safety card availability?
2. Does your cane, frame or other mobility equipment fit the cabin, or will it travel in the hold, and what does that mean for the moment you land?
3. If you're travelling with a guide dog, have you started the Australian re-entry permit process through BICON, and separately confirmed that country's own entry rules for assistance animals?
4. Have you checked whether your guide dog's planned trip length qualifies for reduced testing under the 45-day exemption, and said so on the application?
5. For any major museum or gallery on your itinerary, have you found its specific accessibility or access program page, and booked an audio-described or touch tour on a date that actually exists — rather than assuming one will be available whenever you happen to turn up?
6. Does your travel insurance policy mention your guide dog at all, and if it's silent, have you asked the insurer directly what happens if the dog needs veterinary care overseas?
7. Have you done a trial run of an audio-described tour at an Australian gallery before you go, so the overseas version is a known quantity rather than a first attempt?

## The Bottom Line

A tick box on a booking form is a request, not a confirmation, and the gap between the two is where a low-vision traveller's day most often goes sideways. Ring the airline and get a name against your file, book the audio-described tour before you book the hotel around it, and if a guide dog is coming with you, start the paperwork for coming home the same week you decide to go. None of it is complicated once you know it exists — which, now that you've read this far, you do.
