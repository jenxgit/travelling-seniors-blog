The first morning I woke in a campervan on the South Island, I did not reach for my phone. I reached for the little curtain over the window, because I could hear the lake before I could see it — a soft slap of water against stones, and somewhere further off, a single bellbird working through its scales. Lake Tekapo lay outside, still holding the last of the night's cold, and it took me a full ten minutes to do anything other than sit with a cup of tea and watch the light change on the water. That, I have come to think, is the entire case for seeing this island from behind the wheel of your own small, slow-moving home. You stop where the morning tells you to.

Australians have an easy head start here. New Zealand drives on the same side of the road, the currency conversion is gentle on the nerves, and Christchurch or Queenstown are barely more than three hours from Sydney or Melbourne. But the South Island rewards a particular kind of travelling — not the tick-the-box version, but the kind where you give a place two nights instead of one, and let the campervan itself set the pace.

## Choosing Your Campervan, and Why It Matters More Than the Route

Before you plan a single day, spend real time on the vehicle. Companies such as Britz, Maui, Jucy and Apollo all run fleets suited to two people, and it is worth paying the modest premium for an automatic — most current campervan stock is automatic, but older, cheaper vans in the budget tier can still turn up as manuals, and there is nothing relaxing about learning a clutch on a one-lane mountain pass. Ask, too, about a "self-contained" certification (look for the blue triangle sticker in the listing). A certified self-contained van carries its own toilet and wastewater tank and is legally allowed to free camp overnight in the many scenic pull-offs the island offers; without it, you are restricted to paid holiday parks and Department of Conservation campsites, which run roughly NZ$15–35 (about A$14–32) a night and are well worth booking ahead in summer regardless.

One-way hire — picking up in Christchurch and dropping off in Queenstown, or vice versa — usually adds a relocation fee, but it saves you retracing the same road twice, and on an island this shaped, that matters.

## A Loop Worth Taking Two and a Half Weeks Over

There is no single "correct" route, but after several passes through the island, this is the loop I keep returning to, built for a leisurely two and a half to three weeks rather than a fortnight sprint:

Start in **Christchurch**, giving the rebuilt city a day before heading north to **Kaikoura**, where whale-watching boats leave from a township close enough to the mountains that you can watch snow and sea in the same glance. From there, wind through to **Nelson** and the **Abel Tasman** coastline for beaches that feel more South Pacific than New Zealand, then track down the wilder West Coast to the **Franz Josef** and **Fox** glaciers. Cut inland to **Wanaka**, a gentler cousin of its more famous neighbour, before spending several unhurried days around **Queenstown** — not for the bungy jumping, unless that is very much your thing, but for the gondola views and lakeside cafés. A day trip (or better, an overnight stay in Te Anau) gets you to **Milford Sound**, and the drive there is reason enough to go regardless of the weather. Finish by heading south to **Dunedin**, with its Scottish-flavoured streets and the wildlife colonies of the Otago Peninsula, before the run back up to Christchurch.

Resist the urge to drive it in reverse just to save a day. Distances on a map are deceptive here: New Zealand's mountain roads are narrow, winding and interrupted by single-lane bridges that require genuine give-way etiquette, so budget three to four hours of driving on any day you move towns, not the flat highway speeds you would expect from the same distance at home.

## What to Know Before You Collect the Keys

New Zealand's no-fault Accident Compensation Corporation (ACC) scheme covers the cost of treatment if you are injured in a road accident, which surprises many Australian visitors — but it does not cover illness, cancellation, lost luggage or the cost of getting you home, so comprehensive travel insurance remains essential rather than optional. If you hold an RACV, NRMA, RAA or similar membership, it is worth asking about reciprocal arrangements with the New Zealand Automobile Association (AA) before you go; some hire companies offer discounts to AA-affiliated members that are easy to miss unless you ask directly.

Fuel is sold by the litre and priced a little higher than most Australian capitals, so factor roughly NZ$2.80–3.00 (about A$2.60–2.80) per litre into your budget, and note that campervans are thirstier than a family sedan. Mobile coverage thins out dramatically once you are south of Wanaka or deep in Fiordland — download offline maps before you leave town, and let this be part of the appeal rather than an inconvenience.

## When to Go

Skip the Australian summer holiday crowds of December and January if you can, when campsites book out weeks ahead and Milford Sound's car park fills by mid-morning. The most rewarding windows are New Zealand's autumn (April–May) and spring (late September–November): the passes are clear of snow, the light is soft, and you will often have a lookout entirely to yourself. Winter (June–August) closes some high routes, including sections of the Milford Road, to chains-required conditions or full closure after snowfall, so it is not the season for a relaxed first visit, however tempting the fireside evenings sound.

## A Few Things Worth Packing

A thermos, for the mornings that call for tea on a fold-out chair before anyone else is awake. A paper map as backup to the phone, for the stretches with no signal at all. Layers, always layers — the island can move through three seasons in a single afternoon, particularly near Queenstown and Fiordland. And patience for the single-lane bridges, most of which are shared with rail traffic and all of which ask you to simply wait your turn, which is, in the end, rather good practice for the whole trip.
