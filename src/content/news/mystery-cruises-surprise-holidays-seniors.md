Many of us know the mystery flight. A coach picks you up, someone in a lanyard tells you nothing, and you find out you're spending the day at the football in Adelaide only once the plane begins its descent. It is, I have always thought, a slightly undignified way to travel. And yet the same instinct — surrender the itinerary, trust the operator — has now found its way onto the water, dressed considerably more elegantly.

Windstar Cruises, the small-ship line long favoured by travellers who find the mega-ships a touch overwhelming, has built an entire booking category around the concept. You know your departure port. You know your arrival port. You know the region and the season. Everything in between — the specific harbours, the "hidden gems," as Windstar rather coyly calls them — stays secret until the crew reveals each stop just 36 hours before you arrive.

## How the Concept Was Born

I am fond of the story behind this, because it has the ring of an accident that became a feature. During an earlier voyage through French Polynesia, a cyclone forced an unplanned reroute to the Marquesas Islands. By every account I've read, guests enjoyed the detour rather more than the scheduled ports they missed. Windstar, sensibly, took note.

## What's on Offer

Three mystery sailings are currently on the books for 2026 and 2027:

A Spanish mystery cruise sails Barcelona to Málaga over eight nights in November 2026, tracing "hidden gems along the Mediterranean coast." A Northern Europe voyage in April 2027 runs London to Amsterdam over seven nights, including a passage beneath Tower Bridge before slipping into a string of undisclosed harbours. And for those weighing up the flight time from Australia, the most convenient of the three is likely the Japan mystery cruise in September 2027 — eleven nights from Hokkaido to Osaka aboard the newly built Star Seeker, both endpoints served by direct or near-direct flights from Australian gateways.

There was also a Caribbean sailing earlier in 2026, a seven-night round trip from San Juan that inaugurated Star Seeker's maiden season, so this is not a one-off marketing stunt but a category Windstar appears committed to repeating.

## Does It Suit a Cautious Traveller?

This is the question I'm asked most, and the honest answer is: rather better than it sounds. The uncertainty is confined to the middle of the journey, not the edges. Your start date, end date, ship, cabin category and general climate are all fixed and bookable in the ordinary way, which means the practicalities that matter most to us — arranging insurance, booking flights with sensible connection times, confirming any dietary or mobility needs with the line in advance — are handled exactly as they would be for any other cruise. Windstar's ships carry well under 350 passengers, considerably fewer on the sailing yachts in the fleet, so tendering and shore excursions tend to be more manageable than on a large ocean liner regardless of which port turns out to be that day's surprise.

What you give up is the pleasure — or, for some of us, the burden — of researching every stop months in advance. What you gain, by every account from those who've sailed one, is a crew visibly enjoying themselves as they choose where to take you, and a certain lightness that comes from not knowing quite what tomorrow holds. At a stage of life when most of our decisions are rather thoroughly planned, there is something to be said for handing one of them over.

Booking through an Australian cruise specialist is advisable, both for the currency conversion and for someone who can talk you through exactly what is and isn't guaranteed before you commit.
