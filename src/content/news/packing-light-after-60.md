I have stood behind a great many readers at check-in counters from Sydney to Singapore, watching them negotiate — sometimes tearfully — with an airline employee over a fourth piece of luggage that has, against all reason, appeared at the counter. It is never one bag too many by accident. It is always a "just in case" bag, a "what if it's cold" bag, and a bag containing, invariably, an iron. Nobody irons anything on holiday. I want that stated plainly, once, for the record.

The two-bag rule is not a minimalist affectation. It is a practical system, refined over years of six-week trips, that lets you travel for a month and a half with exactly the same luggage you would take for a long weekend: one carry-on and one checked case. No more. Here is how it actually works.

## The Two-Bag Rule, Defined

One cabin bag, sized to whatever your airline permits (typically 7 to 10 kilograms and a linear dimension around 115 centimetres combined). One checked case, sized to whatever your fare permits (usually 23 kilograms on a full-service carrier, sometimes less on a budget one). That is the entire allowance, for a trip of six days or six weeks. The volume of luggage does not scale with the length of the trip — only the amount of laundering does.

This surprises people every time. It shouldn't. A fortnight and a month and a half require the same wardrobe; the only difference is how many times you wash it.

## Why More Bags Cost You More Than Money

A third or fourth bag is rarely free, and it is never merely inconvenient — it compounds in ways that catch up with you three cities into the trip.

| Cost | Two Bags | Four Bags |
|---|---|---|
| Checked baggage fees (per sector, budget carriers) | Often included or one flat fee | Excess fees on every sector, sometimes $70–$150 AUD each |
| Physical handling | Manageable alone, one hand free | Requires a trolley, a porter, or a very patient travelling companion |
| Cobblestones, stairs, no-lift guesthouses | An inconvenience | A genuine hazard, particularly on uneven European or Asian streets |
| Risk of loss or damage | Two items to track | Four items to track, four chances something goes missing |
| Overhead locker and taxi boot space | Fits without a fight | Frequently does not fit, causing delay and irritation to everyone behind you |

None of this is about virtue. It is about arithmetic. Every bag beyond the second one is something you must lift, count, insure against, and worry about — for the entire length of the trip, at every station, taxi rank and hotel lobby along the way.

## The System That Makes a Long Trip Fit

1. **Choose one colour palette before you pack a single item.** Navy, grey, white, and one accent colour will let every top pair with every bottom. This alone typically halves what people think they need, because they stop packing "outfits" and start packing components.
2. **Pack for the mildest sensible version of the climate, and plan to launder.** You are not preparing for every possible weather event on the itinerary — you are preparing to do a sink wash or find a laundromat every ten to fourteen days. A travel-size packet of laundry detergent weighs nothing and solves more problems than an extra jumper ever will.
3. **Use packing cubes to compress volume, not to sneak in more volume.** Rolled clothing in cubes buys you space — resist the temptation to fill that space with more clothing rather than leaving it as margin.
4. **Wear your bulkiest items on the plane.** Jackets, jumpers, and the heaviest shoes travel on your body, not in your case. This is not a style statement; it is a weight-allowance loophole that has existed for as long as air travel has had a baggage scale.
5. **Leave roughly a fifth of the checked case empty on the way out.** You will buy things. Everyone does. A case packed to its absolute limit on departure has nowhere to put a bottle of wine from the Barossa or a jumper from a market in Ubud on the way home.
6. **Keep one full change of clothes, your medications, and your chargers in the carry-on, never in the checked case.** Checked luggage goes missing more often than travel insurers would like to advertise, and the reader who arrives in Rome with nothing but the clothes on their back has, in my postbag, always been the reader who put the spare outfit in the case that didn't make the connection.

For a six-week trip, that typically means five to seven tops, two or three bottoms, one layer for warmth, sleepwear, underwear for roughly ten days, and one set of shoes beyond the ones on your feet. It sounds implausibly small until you have done it once. After that, it is difficult to pack any other way.

## What Actually Deserves a Second Case

I will not pretend the two-bag rule is universal, and any columnist who insists otherwise has not travelled with a CPAP machine. Medical equipment — CPAP machines, mobility aids, insulin coolers, and similar items — is legitimately separate from your standard allowance. Most airlines exempt declared medical equipment from the checked or carry-on count entirely, provided you notify them in advance and carry the relevant documentation; it is worth confirming this directly with your airline before you fly, rather than assuming it and being proven wrong at the counter. A CPAP machine is not a fourth bag in the sense this article means. An iron, regrettably, is.

## The Bottom Line

Two bags will get you through a long weekend or a two-month itinerary with equal ease, provided you pack for laundering rather than for every contingency your imagination can produce. It will save you baggage fees, spare your back on the cobblestones, and mean you have a free hand for the thing that actually matters at the end of a long travel day — finding the hotel, rather than wrangling the fourth suitcase into the lift that quite clearly was not built for it.
