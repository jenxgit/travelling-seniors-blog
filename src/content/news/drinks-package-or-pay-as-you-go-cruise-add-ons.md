Every cruise line now sells you the same promise before you've even boarded: stop thinking about the extras, pay one flat fee, and enjoy the cruise. Drinks packages, wifi plans and shore-excursion credits are bundled, bannered and pushed hard at every stage of booking. The promise is convenience. The real question — the one worth five minutes with a calculator before you tick "add to cart" — is whether the number on the package actually beats what you'd spend buying the same things one at a time.

I've pulled together the current Australian pricing for three of the lines carrying the most Aussie retirees this season — Carnival, Princess and Royal Caribbean — checked 20 November 2026, and done the maths on drinks, wifi and shore excursions so you don't have to guess.

## The Drinks Package: It All Comes Down to One Number

Every drinks package, on every line, lives or dies on the same calculation: how many standard drinks you'd actually order on a typical sea day, versus what the package costs per day.

**Current Australian pricing, checked 20 November 2026:**

| Line | Package | Price (AUD, per person/day) | What's included |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carnival | Cheers! | $111 pre-purchase / $119 onboard | Alcohol and non-alcohol drinks up to $25 AUD value each; every adult in the cabin must buy it |
| Carnival | Cheers! Zero Proof | $39.99 pre-purchase / $44.99 onboard | Sodas, mocktails, specialty coffee, juice; doesn't need to cover the whole cabin |
| Princess | Princess Plus | $79 (booked 96+ hours ahead) | Drinks up to $22 AUD each, plus wifi, specialty coffee and more |
| Princess | Princess Premier | $129 (booked 96+ hours ahead) | Drinks up to $30 AUD each, premium wifi, specialty dining credit and more |
| Royal Caribbean | Deluxe Beverage Package | Dynamic, roughly $90–$190 | Most drinks up to a per-item cap; price varies by sailing, so check your own cruise planner |
| Royal Caribbean | Refreshment Package | Roughly $50–$60 | Non-alcoholic drinks, mocktails and specialty coffee |

Take Carnival's Cheers! as the worked example, because it has a fixed price. At $111 a day pre-purchased, and with a typical onboard cocktail or beer running somewhere around $12 to $16 under the $25 cap, the break-even point lands at roughly **seven to eight standard drinks a day** — a figure that holds up almost identically on Princess and Royal Caribbean too, once you run their own price caps through the same sum. If you're someone who has a glass of wine with dinner and perhaps one more drink by the pool, you're nowhere near that number, and paying as you go will leave you ahead by a wide margin over a seven-night cruise.

Two catches worth knowing before you dismiss the package outright. First, Carnival and Royal Caribbean require every adult in the cabin to buy the alcohol package together — so if you're travelling as a couple and only one of you drinks, the real daily cost effectively doubles for the drinker alone. Second, pricing in Australia is quoted in AUD with gratuities already folded in; it's the American-dollar figures you'll sometimes see quoted online, with an 18–20% service charge bolted on separately, that don't match what you'll actually see in an Australian cruise planner.

The non-alcohol packages make for an easier sum: at roughly $40 to $60 a day, Zero Proof or Refreshment packages are worth it only if you're genuinely getting through four or more specialty coffees, mocktails or sodas daily. For an occasional soft drink with lunch, the a la carte bill will almost always come in lower.

## Wifi: The Bundle Is Often Cheaper Than Buying It Alone

This is where the maths gets genuinely interesting, because on two of the three lines, wifi isn't really sold on its own at a price worth comparing — it's folded into a bundle that beats buying the pieces separately.

Carnival's Essentials Value Package, for instance, runs $60 a day and bundles the Cheers! Zero Proof drinks package with its Value wifi plan, a $75 AUD shore-excursion discount and a $35 AUD dining discount. Add up the Zero Proof package alone ($39.99) and a standalone Value wifi plan (commonly in the region of $30 a day once converted to AUD, though it's worth confirming the exact current rate in your own cruise planner) and you're already close to $70 before the shore-excursion and dining credits are even counted — making the $60 bundle the better buy for most couples who'd want both anyway.

Royal Caribbean doesn't bundle in the same way on most Australian sailings: wifi is bought separately from the drinks package, with per-device pricing that varies by ship and tends to sit higher than Carnival's for a comparable browsing-and-streaming plan.

If all you actually want is to check messages and photos once or twice a day rather than stream anything, the cheapest single-device plan on any line will cover it — there's rarely a reason to pay for the premium streaming tier unless you're planning video calls home. And on sea days when you'd genuinely rather not be reachable, remember the package can sometimes be cancelled partway through the cruise on request if you decide you don't need it after all — worth asking at the internet desk rather than assuming you're locked in.

## Shore Excursions: What the 20–40% Really Buys You

Ship-sponsored shore excursions typically run anywhere from $50 to $200-plus per person, depending on the activity, with premium or exclusive experiences climbing higher again. Independent operators — a private guide, a small local tour company, a day booked directly rather than through the ship — are commonly 20 to 40% cheaper for a broadly similar experience.

Run the numbers on a couple taking one $150-per-person ship excursion at a port: that's $300 for the two of you. The same tour, or something very close to it, booked independently at a 25% discount works out around $225 — a saving of roughly $75 at that single stop. Do that at half the ports on a ten-port cruise and you're looking at several hundred dollars back in your pocket by the end of the trip.

![A small group of travellers walking along a cobblestone street in an old European town](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1645400961516-b129cea1ab95?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1600&h=900&q=80 "The saving on an independent tour is real — the trade-off is that it's on you to get back to the ship on time")

What that 20 to 40% actually buys when you stick with the ship's own excursion is simple but genuine: if the tour runs late because of traffic or a delay, the ship waits. Book independently and miss the departure, and you're arranging your own way to the next port, at your own cost. That's not a reason to avoid independent tours altogether — it's a reason to be selective about where you use them. A gentle city walk you've done your own research on, in a port with good onward transport links, is a sensible place to go independent. A tight connection, an unfamiliar country, or a port where the ship is your only reliable way home by evening is where the cruise line's premium buys real peace of mind, not just a markup.

## The Bottom Line

None of these add-ons are a trap, and none of them are automatically worth it either — they're a straightforward sum once you know your own habits. Count your actual drinks before you buy the package. Check whether the bundle beats the sum of its parts before you buy wifi on its own. And weigh the real cost of a missed departure against the genuine saving before you wave off every ship excursion in favour of booking it yourself. The cruise lines have done the marketing. The maths is still yours to do.

This is general information to help you compare your options, not financial advice — prices and inclusions change between sailings, so the one figure worth confirming for yourself is whatever your own cruise planner shows before you pay.
