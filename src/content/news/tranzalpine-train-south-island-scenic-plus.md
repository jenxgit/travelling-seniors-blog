Our Destinations correspondent Wren recently sent readers across New Zealand's South Island behind the wheel of a campervan, and I don't begrudge her the romance of it — there is something to be said for stopping wherever the morning tells you to. But a good campervan loop still asks someone in the front seat to navigate single-lane bridges shared with rail traffic, hairpin bends through Arthur's Pass, and the occasional stretch of fog thick enough to make you wish you'd stayed in bed. There is another way to make the same crossing, and it involves nothing more strenuous than deciding what to order from the drinks trolley.

The TranzAlpine runs daily between Christchurch and Greymouth, and in just under five hours it does something a hire car never quite manages: it lets you watch the Southern Alps arrive, rather than having to watch the road.

## The Route, and Why It's Worth the Window Seat

The journey covers 223.8 kilometres one way, departing Christchurch mid-morning and threading out across the flat Canterbury Plains before the Waimakariri River takes over as your travelling companion. You'll cross its gorge on the Staircase viaduct, a good 73 metres above the water, before the line properly begins to climb into the Southern Alps through Arthur's Pass National Park. The train runs through the 8.6-kilometre Otira Tunnel and emerges into a different world entirely — beech rainforest, waterfalls tumbling close enough to the track to catch the light, and the kind of green you simply don't get on the eastern side of the divide. From there it's a gentler run down to Greymouth and the West Coast.

Most carriages have large panoramic windows and roof-level skylights besides, with most seats facing the direction of travel and a power point at every seat — handy if, like me, you can't resist narrating the scenery into your phone for family back home.

## Scenic or Scenic Plus: What You're Actually Choosing Between

KiwiRail's Great Journeys New Zealand runs two classes on the TranzAlpine, and the gap between them is bigger than a simple upgrade.

**Scenic** is the standard, perfectly comfortable fare: panoramic windows, a café-bar car where you can buy food and drink to take back to your seat, and an open-air viewing carriage for photographs without glass in the way (children need an adult with them out there). A one-way adult fare runs to roughly NZ$239, which converts to around AUD $194 at the exchange rate in early November 2026 — check the current rate before you book, since it moves.

**Scenic Plus** is a different experience altogether, and it's had a proper refresh since KiwiRail unveiled the format: a dedicated premium carriage with wide, reclining seats and its own open-air car, where a seasonal menu built from South Island produce is cooked and served at your seat, paired with a Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc or an Otago Pinot Noir depending on the course. Think eggs Benedict on a potato-herb rösti if you're travelling the morning service, or slow-braised Canterbury lamb with wine jus later in the day. It runs to around NZ$499 one way — roughly AUD $404 — and it is, without question, the more memorable way to make this crossing. It won't suit a tight budget, but if you're marking a special trip or simply decided long ago that the holiday is the thing you spend on, I'd book it without hesitation.

Both classes run on every daily service, and KiwiRail has been adding Scenic Plus capacity as demand has grown, so don't assume it's only available on certain days — but do book ahead regardless, particularly over the December-to-February peak, when seats in the premium carriage can sell out months in advance.

## Booking, Seats and a Few Practical Notes

Fares and timetables move, so ring KiwiRail's automated timetable line (0800 872 467 within New Zealand, +64 4 495 0775 from Australia) or check greatjourneysnz.com for the current day's departure times before you lock anything in — the booking team can take phone reservations Monday to Friday on the same number. Seats are allocated by the train manager ahead of departure rather than chosen at the time of booking, so if you want to sit together with travelling companions, call at least a day ahead with your booking reference and ask.

The service runs daily year-round, with the only scheduled gaps being Christmas Day and the days around Labour Weekend in late October — worth checking against your own dates if you're travelling in that window. Heavy luggage travels in the baggage van rather than with you in the carriage, so pack an overnight bag's worth of hand luggage for the journey itself.

### Who This Suits

Anyone who loved the idea of Wren's campervan loop but would rather not be the one white-knuckling it through Arthur's Pass; retirees travelling without a car at all, who still want to see the Alps properly rather than fly over them; and anyone who simply enjoys a long, unhurried lunch with a view that keeps changing outside the window.

### Who Should Skip It

If what you actually want is to stop every twenty minutes for a photo, a short walk or a roadside pie, you're better served by Wren's self-drive route — the train goes where the timetable says, not where you fancy. And if mobility is a significant consideration, call KiwiRail directly before booking to confirm what's available at each station; the operator's accessibility details aren't published in detail online, so it's worth asking the question directly rather than assuming.

### Before You Book

- Ring the automated timetable line or check greatjourneysnz.com for the current day's departure time — don't rely on an old schedule.
- Decide on Scenic or Scenic Plus before you call, since the premium carriage sells out well ahead over summer.
- If travelling with companions, phone to request matching seats at least a day before departure.
- Check the current NZD–AUD exchange rate before budgeting; it was sitting at roughly 81 Australian cents to the New Zealand dollar in early November 2026, but moves regularly.
- Pack hand luggage only for the carriage itself — anything heavier goes in the baggage van.

Wren's campervan takes the scenic route at its own pace. The TranzAlpine takes the same mountains at a fixed hour and hands you a glass of Pinot Noir while it does it. I know which one I'm not doing the driving for.
