More of us are choosing the open road these days. Fill the tank, drive a few hours, discover a town we've never bothered to stop in — it is a gentler kind of travel than an airport queue, and I understand its appeal completely. But I want to spend a little time on something we rarely discuss before setting off: how our eyes experience that road differently than they did twenty years ago.

This is not a cause for alarm, only for attention. The changes are ordinary and near-universal, and understanding them is the first step toward driving with more comfort and confidence, not less.

## What Actually Changes

The lens inside the eye gradually stiffens and becomes slightly less transparent with age — a normal process, though it accelerates if cataracts develop. Two practical consequences follow. First, light scatters more as it passes through, which is what produces that halo effect around headlights and streetlights at night. Second, the pupil doesn't widen as readily in low light, so less light reaches the retina to begin with, right at the moment scattered glare is asking more of it. Add to this a slower adjustment when moving between bright and dark — say, emerging from a tunnel, or meeting oncoming headlights on a dark country road — and the combination explains why so many of us feel a driving trip is more tiring after dark than it once was.

Modern LED headlights don't help matters. Their light is more intense and blue-rich than the older halogen bulbs most of us learned to drive under, which is part of why oncoming traffic at night can feel harsher than it used to.

## What Genuinely Helps

None of this requires giving up the road trip. A few adjustments make a real, measurable difference:

For daytime glare, look for sunglasses that meet the Australian/New Zealand standard AS/NZS 1067:2003, with a lens category of at least 2 and preferably 3 for genuine UV and brightness protection. Grey-tinted lenses remain the most common choice in Australia, cutting brightness evenly without distorting colours you need to read road signs accurately. If you live with glaucoma or early macular changes, ask your optometrist about photosensitive lenses that darken automatically — useful for the in-and-out driving of a road trip through open country and shaded forest alike.

For night driving specifically, resist the instinct to reach for tinted or yellow "night driving glasses." Most optometrists now recommend the opposite: clear lenses with a good anti-reflective coating, which reduces the glare and halo effect from oncoming headlights without further dimming an already low-light scene.

![Sunglasses reflecting soft, colourful bokeh light, illustrating the halo and glare effect that ageing eyes notice more at night](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1514446945-952d86c3449b?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1600&h=900&q=80)

A few habits matter just as much as eyewear. Keep the windscreen properly clean inside and out — a light film that's invisible in daylight becomes a glare-scattering haze the moment headlights hit it. Replace wiper blades before they streak. Have headlights checked for alignment at your next service, since a misaimed globe dazzles oncoming drivers as much as it fails to light your own way. And on the road itself, look toward the left edge of your lane rather than directly at an approaching car's headlights, leave a longer following distance after dusk, and simply drive a touch slower on unfamiliar or poorly lit stretches. There is no schedule worth rushing on a trip you took up for the pleasure of it.

## When to See Someone

If glare has started to feel newly uncomfortable, or night driving has begun to worry you in a way it didn't a year or two ago, that's worth mentioning at your next optometrist visit rather than working around quietly. Ask specifically about contrast sensitivity and glare testing — a standard vision chart can miss changes that only show up under realistic driving conditions. An annual check from your fifties onward is a sensible habit, and if cataracts are found, they are, happily, one of the most reliably treatable conditions in all of medicine.

Your GP or optometrist knows your eyes and your health history; I can only offer the general picture. Use this as a prompt for that conversation, not a substitute for it.
