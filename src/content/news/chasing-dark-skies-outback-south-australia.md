I turned the car's headlights off a little way out from the homestead, mostly as an experiment, and stood there for a moment letting my eyes adjust to what felt at first like nothing at all. Then, gradually, the nothing turned into everything: a sky so thick with stars that the familiar constellations I'd gone looking for got lost in the crowd, and a pale smudge overhead that took me a beat too long to recognise as the Milky Way, because I had simply never seen it look that solid before. I'd driven a long way north from Adelaide to find a sky this dark. It turned out to be the easiest part of the whole trip.

South Australia's outback holds two of the best reasons in the country to look up rather than out, and they suit two rather different kinds of traveller. One is a serious commitment — a proper expedition into the far north. The other is well within reach of a long weekend. Both ask for very little walking once you've arrived, which, for an evening built entirely around sitting still and tilting your head back, is really the whole point.

## Two Dark Skies, Two Different Trips

**Arkaroola Wilderness Sanctuary**, deep in the northern Flinders Ranges, was declared an International Dark Sky Sanctuary in 2023 — only the second place in South Australia, and one of a small number of places on Earth, to earn that particular protection. The designation means the night itself is treated as something worth conserving, with lighting rules written into how the sanctuary operates. The reward is a sky so free of artificial glow that the arid climate and high elevation do the rest of the work for you, and the Milky Way sits overhead for most of the year rather than hiding behind haze.

**Ikara-Flinders Ranges National Park**, and specifically Wilpena Pound, is the gentler alternative, both geographically and logistically. It doesn't carry an official dark-sky designation, but the same dry air and distance from any real town give it strikingly similar conditions on a clear night, and locals will tell you the pound's valley floor is the spot to be. It's also, crucially, several hours closer to Adelaide and reached on a mostly sealed road, which matters more than it sounds once you're the one driving.

I'd choose between them based on how much outback you actually want. If a long, remote drive and a night or two somewhere properly off the grid sounds like the trip rather than an obstacle to it, Arkaroola is where to go. If you'd rather drive five hours, check into a comfortable resort, and still end up under a sky this good, Wilpena Pound does that without asking you to rough it at all.

## Getting There From Adelaide

Arkaroola sits around 600 kilometres north of Adelaide, and the honest answer on driving time is closer to eight hours than six, not because the distance is unreasonable but because the last stretch — roughly 130 kilometres beyond Copley, past Leigh Creek — is unsealed. It's generally well maintained, but check with your hire company before you go: a good many rental agreements exclude dirt roads altogether or charge heavily if you take one, so it's worth sorting out before you're the one explaining a gravel-rash duco to someone at a counter. Travellers who'd rather not drive that last leg themselves can fly or charter in to the Arkaroola airstrip, with pickups arranged from Leigh Creek, Hawker, Port Augusta or Broken Hill — worth asking about directly when you book, particularly if a long unsealed drive isn't how you want to spend a day of your trip.

Wilpena Pound, by contrast, is a comparatively simple five-hour drive from Adelaide up the Princes Highway and through Hawker, almost all of it sealed. It's the kind of trip you can do in a normal hire car without a second thought, and the kind of distance that still leaves you with an afternoon to settle in before the sky does anything interesting.

## What an Evening Under the Stars Actually Involves

This is the part I most want to reassure you about: almost none of it is walking. Arkaroola's "Under the Stars" session is built entirely around sitting in a reclining chair outdoors after dark while a guide talks you through what you're looking at, using astronomy binoculars rather than anything you need to crouch over or squint through. A standard chair runs $65 for an adult, with an upgraded, motorised chair that tilts and turns to follow whatever the guide is pointing out for $90. Sessions are timed for the week either side of the new moon, when the sky is darkest, so it's worth booking around that window rather than a fixed date on the calendar. For anyone who wants to go further, Arkaroola also runs access to the Ningana Imaging Observatory for a proper telescope-based look at the sky, and a ridgetop sleepout for those happy to spend the whole night out under it in a swag.

At Wilpena Pound, the equivalent evening is less formal: find a spot on the valley floor away from the resort's lights, settle in, and let your eyes do the adjusting. Rawnsley Park Station, just south of the pound, also runs heli-camping evenings on the Chace Range for those who'd like the view from somewhere genuinely elevated, with a camp-oven dinner thrown in. Either way, the accommodation itself asks nothing athletic of you — Arkaroola has motel-style lodges with en suites and air conditioning if a swag under the stars isn't your idea of a comfortable night, and Wilpena Pound Resort offers proper rooms a short, flat walk from the valley floor.

## When to Go, and the One Thing Worth Knowing About Aurora

Late spring through early summer suits this trip well, before the real outback heat sets in around December, with clear, settled skies and nights that are cold rather than bitter. If you want to time a visit around the darkest possible sky, the new moon falls on 9 November and again on 9 December this year, and Arkaroola's own stargazing bookings cluster around exactly those windows for good reason.

A word on aurora, because it comes up: the current solar cycle has been an active one, and the Southern Lights have been turning up further north than usual over the past couple of years, occasionally reaching as far as Victoria and even parts of South Australia during the strongest geomagnetic storms. The Flinders Ranges sit well north of where sightings are reliable, so I wouldn't build a trip around seeing them — but on the right night, with a strong enough storm and the Bureau of Meteorology's space weather alerts in your favour, it isn't impossible. Treat it as a happy bonus to a sky that's already worth the drive for the Milky Way alone, not the main event.

## Before You Go

Pack properly for the temperature swing rather than the daytime forecast — desert nights drop sharply once the sun's gone, even in late spring, so bring a proper jacket, a beanie and gloves, and a blanket if you're booking the Arkaroola experience. A torch with a red-light setting is a small kindness to everyone else's night vision, your own included. And if you're driving yourself to Arkaroola, download offline maps before you leave Leigh Creek — mobile coverage disappears well before the sky gets interesting, and a paper map in the glovebox has never once let anyone down.

None of this asks much of you beyond patience and a clear night. Turn the headlights off, give your eyes a few minutes, and let the outback do something it has been doing for rather longer than any of us have been around to watch it.
