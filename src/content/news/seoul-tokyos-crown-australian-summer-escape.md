A friend rang me from a footpath in Shibuya in October, shouting slightly over the crowd noise, to ask whether I thought Tokyo was "just always like this now." I didn't have a tidy answer for her at the time, but I've since had one handed to me by nearly everyone I know who's travelled to Asia this year: no, it isn't always like this — but increasingly, people are going somewhere else instead. That somewhere else, for a growing number of Australian retirees, is Seoul.

It's an odd thing to say about a city of ten million people, but Seoul manages to feel roomier than Tokyo at the moment, and there are real reasons behind that feeling rather than just a traveller's wishful thinking.

## Why Seoul, Why Now

Start with the numbers, because they're more dramatic than I expected when I went looking. South Korea logged 15.05 million international arrivals in the first eight months of this year, up 21.6 per cent on the same period last year and the highest figure the country has recorded in three decades. Australian visits to Korea were already running 11 per cent ahead of the year before as of last August, and 2024's Australian arrivals sat 41 per cent above pre-pandemic levels — this well before this year's growth is even counted. Travel insurer PassportCard reported Korea policy sales up 47 per cent over a six-month stretch, against a 30 per cent *decline* in Japan over the same window, with one of its executives putting it plainly: Japan, Vietnam and Thailand have long been the reliable favourites, but Korea is "really having a moment."

Some of that is cultural. K-pop, K-beauty and K-drama have done more for South Korean tourism than any campaign could have planned, and the Korea Tourism Organisation points to things as specific as a single wildly popular Netflix film pulling curious travellers towards Korean barbecue, street markets and the cafés they've seen on their screens.

But a fair share of it is also simply about Tokyo getting harder to love casually. Japan has spent 2026 raising the cost and friction of a visit in several directions at once, mostly in response to genuine overcrowding: a dual-pricing system is rolling out at major attractions that will quietly charge foreign visitors more than residents, Kyoto has lifted its accommodation tax steeply from March, tax-free shopping at the till ended for tourists from 1 November, and the departure tax every traveller already pays on the way out is set for its first increase since 2019 — reportedly by a considerable multiple. None of this is catastrophic on its own, but stacked together, and stacked against the daily experience of queuing for a shrine or a ramen shop with several thousand of your closest strangers, it's easy to see why Seoul is starting to look like the simpler, calmer choice for a first Asian city break.

## Getting There Has Never Been Easier

This is the part that used to make Korea a harder sell than Japan for Australians, and it has quietly stopped being true. Qantas returned to Seoul in late 2022 after a fifteen-year absence, running direct Sydney-Incheon services, while Jetstar operates its own direct Sydney-Seoul route several times a week on wide-body Dreamliners at considerably friendlier fares — between the two, Sydney travellers now have a genuine choice between a full-service and a budget direct flight, something Tokyo-bound Australians have had for years and Seoul-bound ones have not, until recently.

Melbourne gets its own boost this summer: Korean Air is launching a seasonal nonstop Seoul-Melbourne service running three times a week from 18 December 2026 through to mid-March 2027, timed deliberately around the Australian summer holiday window. It's worth booking around that timetable if Melbourne is your departure point, since outside that season you're generally routing through Sydney or via a Southeast Asian hub.

On paperwork, Australians currently don't need a K-ETA (Korea's electronic travel authorisation) at all — that exemption has been extended repeatedly since 2023 and remains in place through this year — but it's the kind of rule that has moved before and could move again, so it's worth a quick check of the official Korean immigration site a few weeks out from departure rather than assuming nothing has changed since you last looked.

## A Gentle First Few Days

Seoul rewards an unhurried visitor more than its reputation as a hyper-modern mega-city might suggest, and a surprising amount of what's worth seeing sits close together in the old northern part of the city.

**Gyeongbokgung Palace** is the obvious and correct place to start — a sprawling, beautifully restored royal palace with wide, flat courtyards that make for easy, mostly level walking, interrupted by very little in the way of stairs until you choose to seek them out. Time your visit for one of the daily changing-of-the-guard ceremonies at the main Gwanghwamun Gate, a formal, unhurried re-enactment in full period costume that asks nothing more of you than finding a spot to stand and watch. Rent a hanbok nearby for the afternoon if the mood strikes — doing so gets you free palace entry, and nobody at a Korean palace blinks at a visitor well past sixty wearing one.

![Visitors walking through the grounds of Gyeongbokgung Palace in Seoul, with its traditional pavilions in the background](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1591273688202-4cfb3531c7a0?fm=jpg&q=60&w=1600&h=900&auto=format&fit=crop "Gyeongbokgung's courtyards are wide, flat, and unhurried by design")

From the palace, **Bukchon Hanok Village** is a short taxi ride or a longer, gently uphill walk away — a still-lived-in neighbourhood of traditional wooden hanok houses on narrow lanes that climb the hillside in places, so pace yourself and treat it as a slow wander with photo stops rather than a route to complete. A few of the steeper lanes near the top viewpoints are worth skipping if hills aren't your thing; the lower streets are just as photogenic and considerably flatter.

For something warmer and more sociable, **Insadong**, Seoul's old antiques-and-teahouses quarter, is flat, compact and easy underfoot, strung with calligraphy shops, ceramic galleries and tearooms where you can sit for as long as you like over a pot of ginseng or citron tea. **Gwangjang Market**, a little further south, is Seoul's oldest covered market and the best place I know for trying Korean street food without standing in the cold to do it — bindaetteok (mung bean pancakes) and mayak gimbap are the two dishes locals will tell you to order, and the market's covered, heated aisles mean you can eat sitting down out of the weather entirely.

![A busy Insadong street in Seoul lit up with shopfront signs in the evening](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1748389787492-9715be39c210?fm=jpg&q=60&w=1600&h=900&auto=format&fit=crop "Insadong stays flat, lively and easy to wander well after dark")

If you want the skyline view without the climb, the **Namsan Cable Car** carries you most of the way up to N Seoul Tower, leaving only a short, paved stretch of walking at the top — a far gentler proposition than it looks from the ground.

## Seoul in the Cold, and Why That's the Point

An Australian summer break means a Korean winter, and it's worth being honest about what that means: Seoul in December and January sits mostly between zero and minus five degrees, dry and genuinely cold rather than damp, with the odd heavier cold snap. The upside is that Korea does cold-weather infrastructure extremely well — heated floors and underfloor warmth are standard in cafés, restaurants and plenty of indoor waiting areas, and the subway itself runs warm, frequent and easy to navigate even for a first-time visitor, with English signage throughout and lifts at every major station.

It also happens to be festival season. The **DDP light show** at Dongdaemun Design Plaza runs free most winter evenings through the Christmas and New Year period, a ten-minute projection onto the building's striking curved exterior that you watch standing or seated nearby, no walking required. Christmas markets pop up at Gwanghwamun Square and around Lotte World from late November, and Seoul's famous street food carts lean into the season with roasted sweet potatoes and hotteok (a sweet, pan-fried filled pancake) sold hot into your hands, which is about as good as a cold evening gets.

The one genuine caution for winter: footpaths and the stone-paved courtyards at some palaces and markets can turn icy after rain or a cold snap, so proper grip-soled shoes matter more here than they would on a Mediterranean summer trip, and it's worth taking stairs and steps a little slower than you normally would.

## Before You Book

Korea uses the won, and while the exchange rate moves around, most sit-down meals, museum entries and transport fares land cheaper than their Tokyo equivalent at the moment — a welcome change if your last Asian city break was in Japan. Standard travel insurance generally covers South Korea without any special arrangement, but as always, it's worth a call to your own insurer to confirm, and a quick check-in with your GP before any long-haul flight is just sensible practice at any age. Pack for genuine cold rather than a mild Australian winter — a proper coat, gloves and a beanie, not just a light jacket — and you'll find Seoul one of the easier, better-run first steps into Asian city travel that's currently on offer.

Tokyo isn't going anywhere, and it will still be there once the current fuss settles. But for a first visit, or a simpler one, Seoul is making a genuinely good case for itself this year — and for once, getting there is the easy part.
