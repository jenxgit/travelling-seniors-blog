Here's a number worth sitting with before your next big lap: the Caravan Industry Association of Australia puts the average New South Wales powered site at **$58 a night**. The council-run caravan park at Crookwell, in the Southern Tablelands, charges **$28** for the same thing — a powered site for two — and visitor numbers there have jumped 138% in the past year as word has got around. Same category of site, same two adults, same power point. The only thing that changed is which gate you drove through.

## Why the Same Night's Stay Can Cost Double

Big private holiday park chains carry the cost of water parks, jumping pillows, camp kitchens and a marketing department, and that cost sits in the nightly rate whether you use any of it or not. A council-run park, by contrast, usually exists to keep a small town's caravan trade alive and its budget balanced — not to turn a profit — so the fee only has to cover mowing, bins and basic amenities. The trade-off is fewer bells and whistles. For a grey nomad who wants a clean site, a hot shower and somewhere flat to park, that trade-off is almost always worth taking.

**Three worth knowing about, checked 1 November 2026:**

- **Crookwell Caravan Park, NSW Southern Tablelands** — $28 a night for a powered site for two, against the state average of $58. The park was running at 85% capacity in April, with most guests aged 55 to 75.
- **Kyogle Gardens Caravan Park, NSW Northern Rivers** — $29 a night powered for two, dropping to $24 for Campervan and Motorhome Club of Australia (CMCA) members, $15 unpowered, with each extra adult $5 and infants under two free.
- **Wedderburn Pioneer Caravan Park, Loddon Shire, Victoria** — $20 a night powered, $15 unpowered, with a $5 surcharge only if more than two people are staying.

None of these are secret — they're set by public council resolutions — but they rarely show up ahead of the big booking-site listings unless you search the town's own council website directly. That extra five minutes of searching is where the saving actually lives.

## The Two Apps, and Which One Actually Earns Its Keep

**CamperMate** is free to download with no in-app purchases, and it does the basic job well: showing free camps, low-cost council parks, fuel prices and dump points on a map as you drive.

**WikiCamps Australia** costs **$9.99** as a one-off purchase covering every device you own on that platform — not a subscription, and not a recurring charge. What you get for it is a much deeper layer of recent, crowd-sourced reviews and photos, which matters more than it sounds: a free camp that was perfectly legal and lovely eighteen months ago can have new signage, a council crackdown or a washed-out access track today, and WikiCamps' comments are usually the first place that shows up.

Rosie's honest take: start with CamperMate, which costs nothing and covers the essentials. If you're planning a trip longer than a few weeks, the $9.99 for WikiCamps pays for itself the first time its reviews steer you away from a spot that's quietly stopped being free.

## Free Camping: Where It's Legal, and Where It Isn't

Free camping is genuinely legal across most of Australia — in designated rest areas, many state forests, and plenty of national parks — but "free" and "unregulated" are not the same thing, and the fines for getting it wrong are real:

- **New South Wales:** allowed at designated sites and some national parks; fines run $110–$1,100.
- **Victoria:** allowed at designated free camps and parks; fines typically $150–$300.
- **Queensland:** allowed at rest areas and council-approved spots, with many capped at a **24-hour stay**; fines range $250–$2,669 and enforcement is strict around tourist towns.
- **Western Australia:** allowed at designated roadside spots and some remote parks; fines can reach $1,000 or more, especially near Perth and the south-west coast.
- **South Australia:** allowed at approved free camps and roadside stops; fines typically $180–$500.
- **Tasmania:** one of the most van-friendly states, though some sites are marked "self-contained vehicles only," meaning you need your own toilet and greywater storage; fines where they apply run $150–$500.
- **Northern Territory:** allowed at roadside rest areas and designated free camps, with many also capped at 24 hours; fines run $150–$300.

The rule that catches people out isn't usually "can I camp here" — it's "for how long." A spot that's perfectly free for one night can carry a fine the second night if it's signed as a 24-hour stop, so always check the sign at the site itself rather than relying on last season's map pin.

## Five Ways to Stack the Savings

1. **Mix free nights with paid ones.** A week of free rest-area stops followed by two nights at a cheap council park for laundry, power and a proper shower is both more comfortable and still well under half the cost of a week in private holiday parks.
2. **Join the CMCA if you travel more than a few weeks a year.** The discount at parks like Kyogle Gardens, plus member-only overnight stops at wineries and pubs around the country, usually pays back the membership fee within the first long trip.
3. **Travel just outside peak season.** The same powered site that's full at $58 over school holidays is often both cheaper and easier to book in the quieter weeks either side of it.
4. **Always ask about a concession rate, even if it isn't advertised.** Not every council-run park offers one, but plenty quietly extend a small discount to Pensioner Concession Card or Seniors Card holders that never makes it onto the website — it costs nothing to ask at the office.
5. **Run CamperMate and WikiCamps together if your trip is a long one.** The free app covers the everyday search; the paid app's reviews are what stop a "free camp" turning into an awkward conversation with a ranger.

This is general information to help you compare your options, not financial advice — your own budget, vehicle and comfort needs are the final word on what's right for your trip.

## The Bottom Line

The gap between a $58 private powered site and a $20–$29 council-run one isn't a rounding error over a six-week lap — it can be the difference between affording the trip and not taking it at all. Spend the ten minutes finding the council park's own number before you book through a big aggregator, keep one of the camping apps running, and read the sign before you stay a second night anywhere that calls itself free.
