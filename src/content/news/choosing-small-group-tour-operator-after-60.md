I receive more letters about small-group tours than about almost any other subject, and they arrive in two distinct piles. The first pile is glowing: a fortnight in the Dolomites or the Kimberley with fourteen well-matched strangers who are now, apparently, lifelong friends. The second pile is furious: a "small group" that turned out to be thirty-two people and two buses, a "gentle pace" that involved a four-kilometre uphill walk before morning tea, and a single supplement that appeared, uninvited, three weeks before departure. The tour was rarely the problem. The questions that went unasked before booking almost always were.

A brochure is a piece of marketing, not a contract. The words "small group," "easy pace" and "flexible cancellation" mean whatever the operator has decided they mean this season, and there is no regulator standing over their shoulder insisting on consistency. The fix is not scepticism for its own sake — most operators are perfectly reputable — it is simply asking the right questions in the right order, before your deposit changes hands.

## "Small Group" Is Not a Fixed Number

Ask this question first, because every other answer depends on it: **what is the maximum group size, not the average, and how many guides travel with a group that size?** A company can honestly advertise "small group travel" while running tours of anywhere from eight to forty people, and the experience of those two trips has almost nothing in common.

| Group size | What it typically means | Worth knowing |
|---|---|---|
| 6–12 | Genuinely intimate; one vehicle, one guide | Higher per-person cost; little anonymity if personalities clash |
| 13–20 | The most common "small group" tier | Usually one coach, one guide; still manageable for group meals and toilet stops |
| 21–30+ | Sold as "small group" more often than it should be | Often splits into two guides or sub-groups on-site; ask directly whether it does |
| Fully private | Just you, or your own party | The pace and itinerary genuinely bend around you, at a matching price |

If an operator hesitates to give you a straight maximum number, or answers with an average rather than a cap, treat that as your answer. A confident operator will tell you the exact figure without being asked twice.

## What "Easy Pace" Actually Involves

"Gentle," "leisurely" and "easy pace" are among the least regulated words in the travel industry. I have seen "leisurely walking tour" used to describe both a stroll around a cathedral square and a hike that would have tested a younger version of me. Before you book, ask the operator for three specific things, not adjectives:

1. **The daily walking distance and total elevation gain**, in kilometres and metres, not in minutes. "About twenty minutes of walking" can mean two hundred flat metres or two kilometres of cobblestones on a hillside — ask for the distance.
2. **Whether the itinerary includes free days or rest afternoons**, and how many. A tour with zero downtime across twelve days is not gentle regardless of how flat the terrain is.
3. **What happens to a traveller who can't keep up on a given day.** A well-run small-group operator has an answer ready — a slower sub-group, a driver who waits with the vehicle, an alternative activity — rather than a shrug. An operator with no answer to this question has not thought about it, which tells you something important about how the actual day will go.

If you are managing a hip or knee replacement, a heart condition, or reduced mobility generally, get this in writing from the operator, not just a verbal assurance from a booking agent reading off a script. Follow it up with your own GP if the itinerary description leaves you at all uncertain — that conversation costs you fifteen minutes and potentially saves you a ruined holiday.

## The Single Supplement: Ask Before, Not After

If you're travelling alone, the single supplement is where a genuinely appealing price quietly stops being genuine. It typically adds anywhere from 20 to 100 per cent to the per-person twin-share fare, and operators vary enormously in how transparently they disclose it.

Ask three questions specifically:

- **Is the advertised price per person twin-share, and what is the exact single supplement in dollars, not percentage terms?** A vague "supplement applies" answer is not good enough — get the actual figure for your specific departure.
- **Does the operator offer share-twin matching**, pairing solo travellers of the same gender to avoid the supplement altogether, and what happens if no match is found close to departure? Some operators waive the supplement in that case; others simply charge it at the last minute, which is precisely the unpleasant surprise my postbag is full of.
- **Is the supplement locked in at booking, or can it change** if the tour doesn't reach a minimum number of travellers? A supplement that can move after your deposit is paid is a supplement you should get confirmed in writing.

## Cancellation Policies: Read the Actual Tiers

Every operator's terms and conditions contain a cancellation schedule — a table of dates before departure and the percentage of your fare you forfeit at each one. Almost nobody reads it before booking, and almost everybody wishes they had after a knee gives out six weeks before departure.

At minimum, before paying a deposit, work out:

1. **The exact percentage forfeited at 90, 60, 30 and 14 days out.** These tiers vary significantly between operators covering the same style of trip, and a difference of even one tier can matter enormously if your circumstances change.
2. **Whether the deposit itself is refundable within a short cooling-off window** — a number of reputable Australian-based operators offer this; plenty of others do not, and there's no law requiring it.
3. **Whether travel insurance with cancellation cover is compulsory** for the booking, and if so, confirm the policy you're considering actually covers your specific pre-existing conditions before you rely on it to make cancellation "safe."
4. **What happens if the operator cancels** the departure due to low numbers — full refund, credit only, or an alternative date — and how much notice you're contractually owed.

## A Practical Pre-Booking Checklist

Before you hand over a deposit, get answers — in writing, ideally by email rather than a phone call you'll misremember — to each of the following:

1. Maximum group size for this specific departure, and the guide-to-traveller ratio.
2. Daily walking distances and elevation, plus the number of genuinely free or rest days.
3. The exact single supplement in dollars, and the operator's twin-share matching policy.
4. The full cancellation tier schedule, in percentages and dates.
5. Whether the operator is ATAS-accredited (the Australian Federation of Travel Agents' accreditation scheme), which offers a measure of consumer protection worth checking for.
6. What currency the final invoice is issued in, and whether the AUD price you were quoted is fixed or subject to exchange-rate adjustment before final payment.
7. A reference from a past traveller of a similar age and mobility level to yours, if the operator can provide one — a good operator usually can.

None of these questions are confrontational, whatever the tone of the letters I sometimes receive suggests their authors expected them to be. A reputable operator answers all seven without hesitation, generally within a single email. An operator who deflects, delays, or answers only in adjectives has told you everything you need to know before you've spent a cent.

## The Bottom Line

"Small group," "easy pace" and "flexible cancellation" are marketing phrases until you convert them into numbers — a maximum headcount, a distance in kilometres, a dollar figure, a percentage on a specific date. Get those seven answers in writing before you book, and the operator who gives them to you plainly and promptly is very probably the one who will look after you properly once you're actually on the road.
