Here's the number worth knowing before you tap "Afterpay this trip": since **10 June this year**, buy now, pay later has been treated as credit under Australian law for the first time. For over a decade, Afterpay, Zip and the rest sat outside the rules that govern credit cards and personal loans. Now they don't — and that changes what a retiree booking a holiday on four fortnightly payments is actually signing up for.

## What Actually Changed on 10 June

Amendments to the National Consumer Credit Protection Act brought BNPL providers under the same regulator, ASIC, as every other lender. In practice, that means:

- **Providers must hold an Australian credit licence**, the same requirement a bank or finance company has to meet.
- **Credit checks are now mandatory**, rather than the instant, tick-a-box approval BNPL built its reputation on.
- **Responsible lending obligations apply**, so a provider has to make a reasonable assessment that you can repay before approving you — including, in theory, retirees living on a fixed pension or super drawdown.
- **Repayment history is reported to credit bureaus**, the same as a credit card. A missed BNPL payment can now sit on your credit file.

None of this is small print buried on page nine. It's a genuine shift in what these apps are, and the early data backs that up: documented BNPL hardship cases — people who fall behind and can't catch up — rose from 0.06% of agreements in June last year to 0.24% in March 2026, according to credit reporting figures. Still a small slice of all BNPL users, but four times the rate it was before regulation, and a reminder that a "pay in four" trip is still debt, just interest-free debt if you keep to the schedule.

## Where You Can Actually Use It for a Trip, Checked 13 November 2026

**Qantas** offers Zip at checkout, with a **1.5% fee capped at $22 per passenger on domestic flights** or **$120 on international flights** — you'll need to be approved for Zip before you can select it. **Jetstar** runs Afterpay instead, also with a 1.5% booking fee, for purchases up to $3,000 paid off in four fortnightly instalments over six weeks. **Virgin Australia** offers Zip Money for bookings over a longer term — 3, 6 or 12 months — but it comes with a **$99 one-off establishment fee** and a **$7.95 monthly account-keeping fee** for every month you're carrying a balance, plus the possibility of interest if you don't clear it in time. **AirAsia** also runs Zip over 3, 6 or 12 months for its Australia–Asia network.

Online travel agents are where BNPL first took hold in Australian travel: **Webjet** has offered Afterpay since 2021, and **Flight Centre** offers humm on holiday purchases of $999 or more, with a **$99 annual account fee** and 6–9 month terms. Afterpay's general spending limit for travel sits at **$3,000**, split into four fortnightly, interest-free instalments — useful for a flight or a short package, less useful if you're booking a six-week European cruise and land tour.

## The Maths That Actually Matters

Every BNPL provider will tell you the headline is "no interest." That's true if you pay on time, every time. Where the real cost hides is in the fees around the edges:

| Provider | Typical fee structure | Spending limit | Term |
|---|---|---|---|
| Afterpay | No interest on time; late fees can reach $10 plus $7/week, up to $68 per order, if you miss one | Up to $3,000 for travel | 4 fortnightly payments |
| Zip Pay | Revolving credit; $5/month fee if there's an unpaid balance | Up to $1,000 (standard) | Minimum $40/month repayment |
| Zip Money | $99 establishment fee, $7.95/month account-keeping fee, interest may apply after any promotional period | Higher limits, airline-dependent | 3, 6 or 12 months |
| humm (Flight Centre) | $99 annual account fee | Purchases of $999+ | 6–9 months |

None of these beat simply putting the same instalment amount into your own savings account for the same number of fortnights and paying for the trip outright when you book — you'd earn a little interest instead of risking a fee, and nothing shows up on your credit file either way. Where BNPL genuinely earns its keep is **locking in a sale fare today** that would otherwise sell out or rise in price before you'd saved the full amount — the convenience has a value, it just isn't free.

## Five Questions Worth Asking Before You Book

1. **Am I using this because the fare expires tonight, or just because I can?** A real sale with a real deadline is a reasonable use of instalments. "I'd rather not pay it all at once" is really just a savings plan with extra fees attached.
2. **What happens to my booking if I miss a repayment?** Read the airline or agent's own terms, not just the BNPL provider's — a missed instalment can affect your booking status as well as your account with Afterpay or Zip.
3. **Is the 1.5% surcharge already baked into the advertised fare, or added at checkout?** On a $2,000 return international fare, 1.5% is $30 — worth confirming before you compare it against paying by card.
4. **Will this show up on my credit file, and does that matter to me?** If you're not planning to apply for any other finance — a car loan, a reverse mortgage, a bridging loan for downsizing — a clean repayment history costs you nothing extra to maintain. If you might need to borrow for something else in the next year or two, a missed BNPL payment is no longer invisible to a future lender.
5. **Have I compared it against a low-rate credit card instead?** Several Australian cards now offer 0% introductory periods on new purchases for 6–15 months with no separate account-keeping fee — worth a five-minute comparison before you assume BNPL is automatically the cheaper instalment option.

## If You're Living Off a Fixed Income

The responsible lending rules cut both ways for retirees. On one hand, a provider now has to properly assess whether a pension or super drawdown can sustainably cover the repayments, which is a genuine protection against being approved for more than you should comfortably take on. On the other, it means approval is no longer instant for everyone — some retirees who'd have sailed through the old instant-approval system may find the new credit check asks more questions about income and existing debts than they're used to answering for what feels like a straightforward holiday booking.

If a provider declines you or asks for more detail than expected, that's the new rules working as intended, not a reflection on you personally — and it's worth remembering you were never obliged to use BNPL in the first place; it's one option among several for timing a payment, not the only one.

This is general information to help you compare your options, not financial advice — your own budget, existing debts and plans for any other borrowing are the final word on whether instalment-paying a holiday makes sense for you.

## The Bottom Line

BNPL for travel isn't a scam and it isn't free money — it's now a regulated credit product with real fees, real credit checks, and a real mark on your file if it goes wrong, same as the card already in your wallet. Use it to lock in a fare you'd otherwise miss, read the fee schedule before you tap "approve," and compare it honestly against just saving the instalment amount yourself. Sometimes the four fortnightly payments are worth it. Often, the cheapest plan is still the one where you already have the money before you book.
