I pulled over on the side of the Midlands Road with no real plan to, because the verge had turned a colour I did not have a name for — not quite pink, not quite white, somewhere in between, and moving very slightly in the wind like something breathing. It was a stand of everlastings, the kind that grow wild and uninvited along hundreds of kilometres of Western Australian roadside every spring, and the woman who pulled in behind me a few minutes later, also uninvited, also unable to help herself, said what everyone seems to say the first time: that she had lived in Perth for thirty years and never once made time to drive north and see this properly. Neither, until that week, had I.

Western Australia holds something close to twelve thousand flowering plant species, more than half of them found nowhere else on earth, and each spring an enormous share of them come out at once across a state so large that the bloom does not really behave like one event so much as a slow-moving front — starting in the warm north in the middle of the year and working its way south as the weeks turn cooler, so that by the time one region is finishing, another is only just getting started. That staggered timing is the whole appeal of a Perth-based loop, and also its one real trick: you are not chasing a fixed date on a calendar, you are chasing where the season currently is.

## One Starting Point, Two Directions

Whichever way you go, begin in Perth itself. Kings Park's Everlasting Festival is running now, this year from 18 September through to 4 October, spread across Kings Park and the Botanic Garden with more than three thousand of the state's plant species on display in curated garden beds, guided walks each morning, and — if your visit lines up with the first weekend of October — an after-dark light event through the western end of the park. Entry to Kings Park is free every day of the year, which makes it the easiest possible warm-up: an hour or two among identified, labelled specimens before you go looking for the same flowers growing wild.

From there, the loop worth taking depends on how much of the season is still ahead of you.

## North to the Wildflower Way, While It Lasts

If you can give it three or four unhurried days, point the car north on the Brand Highway. Break the first leg at **New Norcia**, Australia's only monastic town, about ninety minutes out of Perth — the bakery there does a trade in bread made to a recipe the Benedictine monks have kept for well over a century, and it is worth the stop even without a single flower in sight yet.

Push on through **Dalwallinu** and **Wubin**, wheatbelt towns that mark the start of wildflower country proper, before the road brings you to **Coalseam Conservation Park**, about four and a half hours and roughly 400 kilometres north of Perth, 33 kilometres northeast of Mingenew. This is the single most photographed stretch of the entire Wildflower Way, a river valley that turns into a carpet of yellow, pink, purple and white everlastings after a good winter, with banksias, hakeas and grevilleas filling in the gaps. The Miners Campground here is unpowered, with no running water, and during the peak August-to-October rush stays are capped at three nights so the park does not fill solid — book ahead, or plan to base yourself in Mingenew or Mullewa instead and drive in for the day.

A short detour from Mullewa is worth building in for the state's genuine showstopper: the wreath flower, *Lechenaultia macrantha*, which grows almost nowhere else in the world and forms a near-perfect ring of red and white blooms around a bare centre, low to the ground and easy to miss unless you already know to slow down. It flowers anywhere from July through October depending on how the season's rain has fallen, which is the honest truth about all of this — no website, this one included, can promise you an exact date, only a window.

Loop back to Perth via Geraldton and the coast if you would rather see the ocean than retrace the inland road, adding a day but very little driving fatigue, since the coastal run is flat and well sealed the whole way.

## What It Costs and What to Book

Day entry to Western Australia's national parks, including Coalseam and Stirling Range below, runs to $17 per vehicle carrying up to twelve people, or $10 for concession card holders — Seniors Card and Pensioner Concession Card holders both qualify, and if you expect to visit more than a couple of parks in the trip, the concession-rate annual All Parks Pass works out cheaper than paying at each gate. Fuel is the other real cost on a loop this size: expect to cover 700–900 kilometres depending on which route home you take, so budget accordingly and fill up in the larger wheatbelt towns rather than assuming the smaller ones will have a servo open. Accommodation in the small towns along the way is modest and books out fast in peak wildflower weekends, so a room in Mingenew, Mullewa or Dalwallinu wants locking in at least a few weeks ahead rather than left to chance.

Mobile coverage thins out considerably once you are past Dalwallinu, so download offline maps before you leave Perth, and carry a paper map as backup — it is the kind of country where a flat tyre with no signal is more inconvenience than emergency, but only if you were expecting the possibility.

## If You've Left It a Little Late, Go South Instead

Here is the useful part of the bloom's staggered timing: if the northern run above feels like it has already had its moment for this year, the **Stirling Range**, inland from Albany and roughly a four-and-a-half-hour drive southeast of Perth, is only just coming into its best weeks through October and into November. It is a genuinely different kind of wildflower country — cooler, hillier, and home to an extraordinary diversity of orchids and mountain flora found on nowhere else in the state, with Bluff Knoll rising above it all for those who still enjoy a solid walk. Stirling Range Retreat and the Mount Trio Bush Camping and Caravan Park both sit on the park's northern boundary and make a comfortable, unfussy base, and Albany itself, an hour or so further south, offers proper beds and restaurants for anyone who would rather day-trip into the park than camp beside it.

A loop that takes in both directions in one trip is a big ask — the north and the Great Southern are the best part of a day's drive apart from each other — so I would not try to force them into a single itinerary. Better to treat this as two separate wildflower trips, a year or a season apart, and let each one have its own unhurried pace.

## When to Go

As a rough guide for future years: the far north opens the season from around June, the Coral Coast and Wildflower Way peak through August and September, Perth and Kings Park follow through September into early October, and the South West and Great Southern, including the Stirling Range, carry the season through to November. None of it is exact, and a dry winter can shift every date by weeks — so treat this less as a calendar and more as a compass pointing you toward wherever the flowers currently are.

## A Few Things Worth Packing

A wide-brimmed hat and proper sun protection, since much of this driving happens under open sky with very little shade at the stops themselves. A full tank before you leave the last town of any size, and a paper map to go with the phone. Sturdy, closed shoes rather than sandals for walking the trails at Coalseam or into the Stirling Range, where the ground is uneven and the best flowers are rarely right beside the car park. And, as always in a national park, eyes only — it is illegal to pick Western Australia's wildflowers under the Biodiversity Conservation Act, and the ones left growing are the ones the next carful of travellers gets to pull over for as well.

Until the next slow morning somewhere new, — Sofia
