I have a confession: the first time a reader asked me about the Caucasus, I had to check a map. Three countries, wedged between the Black Sea and the Caspian, that most of us can name but couldn't quite place — Georgia, Armenia and Azerbaijan. I have since been twice, and both times I came home slightly bewildered that more of us aren't going.

Picture a monastery carved into a cliff face in the eleventh century, still ringing with the same bells. A grandmother in a village outside Tbilisi pressing homemade churchkhela — walnuts dipped in grape must, dried into a rope — into your hands because you admired her garden. A skyline in Baku that looks like something from a science-fiction film, glass towers shaped like flames rising over a Caspian waterfront. The Caucasus holds all of this within a few hours' drive of itself, which is precisely why small-group operators have quietly built entire itineraries around linking the three countries together.

## Why Now, and Why Together

Georgia, Armenia and Azerbaijan don't get the same coverage as Portugal or the Douro Valley, but that's beginning to change. Australian tour companies including Odyssey Traveller and Travel Directors have both expanded their Caucasus departures for 2026 and beyond, pitched squarely at travellers who've done the well-worn European circuits and want somewhere that still feels like a discovery rather than a queue.

Part of the appeal is how differently the three countries sit beside one another. Georgia is wine country in the truest sense — archaeologists date winemaking there back eight thousand years, and it shows in a food culture built around long, unhurried tables. Armenia is older still in places: Geghard Monastery and the temple at Garni predate most of what we consider ancient Europe. Azerbaijan is the surprise of the three, a country where Soviet-era apartment blocks give way, within a single afternoon's drive, to Baku's ultra-modern waterfront and the fire-worship history that gave the "Land of Fire" its name.

## What a Trip Actually Looks Like

A typical small-group itinerary runs 22 to 23 days and flies out of your choice of Australian capital via Dubai, landing in Baku and finishing in Yerevan (or the reverse), with Tbilisi as the natural midpoint. Odyssey Traveller's version is pitched as "moderate" — up to about 8 kilometres of walking on a full day, some of it on uneven cobblestones or monastery paths — with a maximum group size around 14, small enough that you'll know everyone's name by day three. Expect coach transfers between cities, a cable car up to the hilltop fortress above Tbilisi, and at least one journey by 4WD into the mountains, where a golf cart sometimes takes over for the least mobile members of the group.

Budget realistically. Odyssey's departures start from around **AUD $13,700 per person twin share**, while Travel Directors' more comprehensive 23-day itinerary runs closer to **AUD $18,450**, with a single supplement of roughly $3,150. Both prices are steep, but they include almost everything once you land — most meals, all internal transport, and guides who've usually spent years working these three borders and know precisely which checkpoint queues to avoid.

## When to Go

Skip both extremes. High summer bakes Baku and the lowland stretches of Azerbaijan, while winter closes mountain passes in Georgia's Svaneti region that you'd otherwise want open. The sweet spot is spring (May–June) or, better for most Australians' calendars, **autumn departures in September and October** — you'll be trading our own spring for the Caucasus's gentlest season, with daytime temperatures in the mid-twenties in Georgia and a touch cooler once you're up in Armenia's highlands.

## Before You Book

A few practicalities worth sorting early:

Visas are straightforward for Australian passport holders — Georgia is visa-free, Armenia offers visa-on-arrival or a simple e-visa, and Azerbaijan requires an e-visa applied for online in advance, best done a month out rather than the week before you fly. Travel insurance should specifically cover the region; not every standard policy lists Armenia and Azerbaijan by name, so ring your insurer and ask rather than assume. And because the itinerary crosses land borders between Georgia and Armenia, and separately into Azerbaijan, pack a little patience for paperwork at the crossings — it's part of the texture of the trip, not a flaw in it.

None of this needs to be adventurous in the backpacking sense. It simply needs an open afternoon and a willingness to be somewhere genuinely unfamiliar. That, more than any monastery or mountain view, is what I keep coming back for.
