I last stood at Remarkable Rocks in the summer before the fires, when the car park was gravel and the only sound was wind getting into the granite's hollows. I went back this spring half-expecting a memorial of some kind — a scorched reminder, a sign explaining what was lost. What I found instead was a new boardwalk, a resurfaced road, and a family from Melbourne working out how to get a grandmother in a sun hat close enough to the boulders for a photo without any of them having to scramble over rock. Six years on from the Black Summer bushfires, Kangaroo Island isn't asking visitors to come and see what burned. It's simply open again, and getting on with being itself.

That matters for anyone weighing up a South Australian trip who isn't up for the long haul across the Nullarbor. Kangaroo Island asks for a short ferry ride or a 40-minute flight, not several days behind the wheel, and it gives back granite coastlines, slow food and genuinely good wildlife-watching in return. It also asks for very little walking unless you go looking for more.

## What the Fires Actually Did, and What's Changed Since

The 2019-20 fires burned through roughly half the island — close to 200,000 hectares — and tore through almost all of Flinders Chase National Park, the island's best-known reserve, along with a swathe of private bushland to its south and west. For a while the park was simply closed, and the stories that came out of that summer were grim ones: tens of thousands of the island's koalas killed, most of a small, ground-dwelling marsupial called the Kangaroo Island dunnart's habitat gone, and a population of glossy black-cockatoos found nowhere else in South Australia caught almost entirely inside the fire zone.

What's changed is that none of this is where the story has stayed. Flinders Chase reopened with a new visitor centre on South Coast Road, home to a small café for a coffee and a sandwich before or after you walk. The road out to Remarkable Rocks has been resurfaced, and a new boardwalk now carries you right up to the lichen-crusted granite boulders without a scramble required. May's Homestead and the old Postman's Cottage, both damaged in the fires, have been restored and partly rebuilt, and are bookable now as simple self-contained cabins if you'd rather sleep inside the park than drive back out of it.

Admirals Arch, a short drive further along, is the easiest stop in the whole park for anyone not keen on uneven ground: a boardwalk leads you out above the water to a colony of long-nosed fur seals that haul themselves onto the rocks below in full view, no scrambling or binoculars required. Between the two, you can see most of what the park is known for without more than a few hundred level metres of walking in either direction.

## A Quiet Kind of Wildlife Comeback

The numbers from early 2020 were sobering: an estimated 50,000 koalas on the island before the fires, reduced within months to perhaps a fifth of that, with roughly 85 per cent of their habitat burnt. Glossy black-cockatoos, numbering only around 370 birds in seven flocks before the fires, had most of their population caught inside the fire zone. The dunnart, already rare, lost more than 90 per cent of its known habitat overnight.

Conservation crews have spent the years since doing patient, unglamorous work: nest boxes and she-oak replanting for the cockatoos, camera-trap surveys through the park's unburnt pockets that have confirmed dunnarts are still holding on in Flinders Chase and nearby Ravine des Casoars, and a small disease-free koala population established on the mainland at Cleland Wildlife Park as an insurance policy in case the island's own recovery faltered. None of it makes for a tidy "fully recovered" headline, and I'd treat any number you hear quoted with some caution — the island's own land managers are still monitoring rather than declaring victory. But walk the Platypus Waterholes track in the cool of a morning now and you'll see kangaroos grazing without much concern for your footsteps, echidnas working the leaf litter along the roadside, and bush that has gone from blackened trunks to properly green in a way that would have seemed a long way off in 2020.

At **Seal Bay Conservation Park**, on the island's south coast, the Australian sea lion colony that draws most visitors was never part of the fire story, but it's worth building a day around regardless. A 900-metre boardwalk, fully wheelchair accessible, loops out above the dunes for a self-guided look at the colony from a respectful distance — no booking required, adult entry $20.70, concession (Seniors Card accepted) $16.60, allow half an hour and go either before 11am or after 1pm if you'd rather have it quieter. The guided walk down onto the beach itself, where you'd normally get closer, is paused at the time of writing while the colony pups undisturbed — check current opening status before you plan around it, since it tends to reopen once pupping season passes.

## A Slower Pace for Anyone Who'd Rather Not Hike

If the island's reputation as a hiking destination puts you off, you can ignore it entirely and still see the best of it on sealed roads, short boardwalks and a comfortable pace. The ambitious option exists if you want it: the 66-kilometre Kangaroo Island Wilderness Trail through Flinders Chase has been fully restored since the fires and can be walked over five days independently or with a guide, and a new premium guided option, the Kangaroo Island Signature Walk, now runs four days and three nights through the same country with two guides and nights in those renovated Cape du Couedic lightkeeper's cottages — a proper undertaking, and a pricier one, suited to fit walkers rather than a first outing back on your feet. For everyone else, the short walks at Remarkable Rocks, Admirals Arch, Platypus Waterholes and the Seal Bay boardwalk will show you the same landscape without the multi-day commitment.

## A Slow Food Trail Worth Building a Day Around

![Sheep grazing in a dry, open paddock under a wide sky](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1742208647998-84abde536196?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1600&h=900&q=80 "Grazing country like this covers much of the island's interior between the national park and the coast")

Kangaroo Island has quietly become one of the better places in South Australia for an unhurried afternoon of eating, and most of it is cellar-door and farm-gate simple rather than anything requiring a booking months out. **Island Pure Sheep Dairy** lets you watch the flock before sampling the yoghurt and cheese made from their milk. **Clifford's Honey Farm** keeps Ligurian bees, a strain found almost nowhere else, and will talk you through the hives before you taste the honey. **Kangaroo Island Spirits**, one of the country's early craft gin producers, pours tastings built around native botanicals the makers forage themselves. Down on the water, the **Oyster Farm Shop** does nothing more complicated than fresh local oysters and a view, and the island's handful of cellar doors — among them Bay of Shoals and Islander Estate — are easy, flat-access stops if you'd rather sit with a glass than stand at a tasting bench. None of it asks more of you than deciding how many stops to fit into a day, and a local map from the Kangaroo Island Gateway Visitor Centre at Penneshaw will have the current list of who's open when.

## Getting There, and Getting Around

The ferry from Cape Jervis, about two hours' drive south of Adelaide down the Fleurieu Peninsula, crosses to Penneshaw in around 45 minutes with SeaLink, who run roughly a dozen sailings a day in each direction in season. Fares move with the calendar, but expect somewhere around $49 one way for an adult and $39 concession with a Seniors Card, plus a separate fare if you're bringing a car across — worth checking SeaLink's current prices before you book, since they're reviewed each year. Flying is the other option: both Rex and Qantas currently operate the roughly 40-minute hop from Adelaide to Kingscote, with fares that swing a good deal more than the ferry's, so it pays to compare both before you commit. Once you're on the island, a hire car is genuinely the easiest way to see it — distances are longer than they look on a map, and public transport between towns is limited.

## Before You Go

Pack for sun and wind rather than heat alone — the coastal stretches around Flinders Chase catch a stiff breeze even on a mild day. Book the Admirals Arch and Remarkable Rocks park entry online ahead of a summer visit, since it gets busy over the school holidays. And if six years still sounds recent, remember that the bush doesn't read a calendar the way we do: what looked like ash in 2020 is, on the morning I walked it this spring, just bush again, doing what it's always done.
